\section{Assuring Experience and Trust: QoE, Security and Privacy}\label{sec:assure}

The previous sections optimise delivery against targets. This section asks how we know that a networked
XR system is good enough for its users, and safe for them. The function covers \fnAssureN{} papers
(\fnAssureSh{} of the corpus), and its share rose from \fnAssureEarly{} in 2017--2019 to \fnAssureLate{}
in 2022--2024. Two cells hold it, with opposite evidence profiles. QoE measurement and modelling
(\nxrNProbQoeMeasurementModelling{} papers) is the most user-centred cell in the atlas:
\nxrUsersQoeMeasurementModelling{} of its papers test with users. Security, privacy and trust
(\nxrNProbSecurityPrivacyTrust{} papers) grew \nxrCagrProbSecurityPrivacyTrust{} a year, yet
\nxrNoEvalSecurityPrivacyTrust{} of its papers have no evaluation and \nxrUsersSecurityPrivacyTrust{} test
with users. The argument of this section is that both halves measure the ends of a chain but not its
links. QoE studies relate network impairments to ratings under conditions that real links do not produce,
and security studies measure attacks precisely but never price defences in delay or experience.
\Cref{fig:assure-chain} draws the chain, the attack surfaces and the points where defences add delay.

\subsection{What users weigh}
Users of remote-rendered VR weigh loss above delay. On ALVR, round-trip times (RTT) up to 90~ms were
hardly noticed, while 8\% loss and a 35~Mbps cap degraded experience \cite{W3095344018}. With CloudXR
(N=30), QoE fell at RTT of 75~ms or more, at loss above 6\%, or at 25~ms combined with 4\% loss
\cite{W4402156718}. Loss also outweighed delay for presence and emotion on Quest~3 (N=16)
\cite{W4404916373}. In social XR, RTT up to 77~ms had no significant effect but jitter did
\cite{W4410087219}. The motion-to-photon (MTP) loop explains part of this. Isolated stalls of up to four
frames went unnoticed, and reprojection helped from eight frames (N=29) \cite{W4301184864}.

The latency budget is contested. Requirement papers set 5--10~ms RTT and 20~ms interaction latency from
physiology \cite{W3035467795}, a factor of four to ten below what users tolerated. Client-side
reprojection could reconcile the two, but no study has varied RTT and latency compensation together. A
second weakness concerns the impairments themselves. None of the \nxrSynXCellQoEUsers{} user-tested papers
in the QoE cell replays measured network dynamics; all apply constant RTT, independent loss or throttling,
and real 4G and 5G measurements enter only after the fact, as inputs to models fitted under constant
impairments \cite{W4405935991}. Wireless loss is bursty and correlated, so the thresholds may not transfer.

Other factors are content and group effects. For volumetric content, frame rate dominated in a
102-participant study \cite{W4384703256}, and two quality levels were indistinguishable at 5~m on a
HoloLens~2 (N=32) \cite{W4387831705}. Cybersickness is contested: fewer than 22\% of viewers reported it
for AR point clouds \cite{W4387831705}, and it was ``not an issue'' for 360-degree video
\cite{W4212788331}; content motion and display, not the network, seem to drive it. Group QoE is tested
almost only in pairs \cite{W4415821547}, with the impairment on one link, so group-level effects of who is delayed
remain unknown.

\subsection{Models, standards and traffic}
Objective models are established for coding impairments and unproven for network ones. PSNR tracks
MOS for 360-degree video \cite{W2741833902}, colour PSNR correlated at 0.84 for point
clouds \cite{W4387831705}, and the ITU-T P.1203 and P.1204.3 bitstream models have been extended to tiled
streaming \cite{W3200608537}. Learned models fit well but locally. A cloud VR gaming model
reached PLCC 0.93 and raised interaction quality by 1.20 MOS when used to adapt encoding
\cite{W4400981668}, and regressions on the CloudXR data reached a mean absolute error of 0.14
\cite{W4405935991}; each was fitted and tested on a few contents. Temporal pooling is missing: P.919 uses
10~s stimuli while presence needs multi-minute tasks \cite{W4284879755}, and no model pools sub-second
network events into session QoE. The gap reaches well beyond this cell. ``QoE'' appears in
\nxrSynQoEtermN{} abstracts, of which \nxrSynQoEtermUsersN{} were tested with users, and an atlas screen
with two independent readers found 256 streamed-XR papers, mostly on resource allocation and latency, that
lack an RTT, jitter and loss-to-MOS model yet could use one directly as objective, constraint or evaluator.

Network-side estimators are validated against formulas, not people. PERCEIVE predicted a parametric QoE
index within 4\%, but its authors state that the index was never checked against ratings
\cite{W2809912305}; in-network and data-plane estimators share this ground truth. MetaVRadar classified
metaverse activity from encrypted traffic with 92--100\% true positives without measuring experience
\cite{W4389609035}.

Traffic measurements show that the application stack, not the link, often sets what users see. WebRTC
pacing in 5.56~ms batches set frame delay at 6--21~ms over Wi-Fi~6 with an RTT of 1.5~ms
\cite{W4391506055}. Under 27~Mbps throttling, adaptive bitrate kept cloud VR frame loss at or below 5.12\%
where a fixed 40~Mbps stream lost 32.51\% \cite{W4210470894}. Models fitted to one stack disagree: the
3GPP baseline underestimated effective VR bitrate by 25\% \cite{W4399340506}, and the open RiftCat model
matched mean but not 95th-percentile delay \cite{W3192844644}. Shared worlds need far less than stated:
Horizon Workrooms used 2--3~Mbps downlink and 0.6~Mbps uplink \cite{W4294643390}, far below the
per-user rates that metaverse visions state (\cref{sec:provision}).

\subsection{Attacks on XR traffic}
Encrypted XR traffic leaks what users do, and this result is established across platforms and groups.
Traffic patterns of a mobile AR application located users with 94\% accuracy \cite{W2946752973}. On Meta
Quest Pro, off-the-shelf classifiers identified applications with 92.4\% accuracy and activities with
91\%, from less than 10 minutes of traffic per application \cite{W4406880464}. Offloading AR glasses reveal the type of physical scene through packet
sizes and timing \cite{W7215028895}.

Motion data are the sharpest channel. Avatar motion received by an adversary's own client in multi-user VR
yielded 97.62\% of typed keystrokes, and the attack generalised to four applications
\cite{W4398796475}. The first result
turns state synchronisation, which broadcasts head and hand poses to every peer, into a leakage channel.
Platform audits add the provider as observer: about 70\% of Oculus data flows were not properly disclosed
\cite{W3202887304}.

\subsection{Defences and their uncosted latency}
Defences are proposed far more often than they are measured. Of \nxrSynSecLatN{} corpus papers that
mention both a security term and latency or QoE, \nxrSynSecLatNone{} have no evaluation and
\nxrSynSecLatUsersN{} were tested with users. Authentication schemes for metaverse avatars and
tele-surgery are proven in BAN logic, AVISPA or Real-or-Random models and costed in operation counts
\cite{W4295788720,W3126938564}; no cross-metaverse scheme is measured during the hand-off where
re-authentication falls. ``Metaverse'' intrusion detectors report 96--99\% accuracy on
IoT and enterprise datasets that contain no XR flows \cite{W4388011607}. The exceptions are
line-rate detection in Tofino switches at up to 99\% accuracy with under 5\% of switch resources
\cite{W4387412865} and the first labelled metaverse attack dataset \cite{W7127620891}. Blockchain is
contested: a 10-user pilot kept 88~FPS at 142 transactions per second \cite{W7161842864}, while most
ledger frameworks report no latency at all. Federated learning failed for VR biometric authentication, below 10\% accuracy
\cite{W4378696892}.

The privacy--QoE trade-off is modelled, not perceived. Viewpoint leakage persists under federated
prediction once prediction errors or QoE are uploaded \cite{W4223932369}. Noise remapping reduced the QoE
loss of privacy noise by 55--100\%, to 0--3\% at maximum privacy \cite{W4396941592}, and client-side pose
noise lowered re-identification on real 6DoF traces \cite{W7168293765}; none of the 15 papers in this line
asked users. Selective attribute-based encryption cut encryption and decryption time by up to 50\% and
80\% and kept volumetric content cacheable \cite{W7124239969}. The transport cost is known only at its
extremes. Encrypting 1400-byte packets did not fit a 0.35~ms edge budget \cite{W2923086750}; secured VR
streaming under a flood held about 30--32~ms in simulation \cite{W7161887783}; and padding or jitter alone
did not stop scene leakage, while combined defences conflicted with QoS \cite{W7215028895}. No
real-testbed paper reports a defence's latency together with QoE or frame rate. A defence should be
reported as a point on a privacy--QoE plane: attacker accuracy against added p99 frame latency and
bandwidth, on a real split-rendering pipeline.

\begin{openproblems}
\item \textbf{Network-side QoE estimators are not validated against users.} Estimators predict parametric
  indices, and none targets interactive remote rendering. A public benchmark pairing encrypted packet
  traces, client frame logs and per-minute ratings for cloud VR (N$\geq$30) would let estimators be
  trained and compared.
\item \textbf{XR traffic models are open loop.} Each is fitted to one stack, one user and short captures,
  without head motion or rate adaptation, and errs in the tail. A closed-loop generator from several
  stacks and users, validated against p95 frame delay with 1--12 real headsets, would replace them.
\item \textbf{Defences are not costed against the frame deadline.} No testbed study reports a defence's
  latency with QoE or frame rate. Measuring TLS, DTLS and QUIC with padding, bucketing and jitter on
  split rendering, as attacker accuracy against p99 frame latency, would give providers a price.
\item \textbf{Authentication is validated symbolically, never during hand-off.} Cross-platform schemes
  have no measurements. Implementing published schemes and 5G re-authentication on a headset--edge
  testbed, and counting frames delayed per hand-off, would show which fit the interaction path.
\item \textbf{Intrusion detectors are trained on traffic without XR flows.} One labelled XR attack
  dataset exists. Capturing benign and attack XR traffic on an open 5G testbed and testing published
  detectors across datasets would show whether 96--99\% accuracies transfer.
\item \textbf{Viewport and pose privacy has no user validation.} QoE losses come from prediction accuracy.
  A within-subjects study comparing noise, camouflage and client-side prediction at equal re-identification
  risk, with MOS and sickness scores, would show what users notice.
\end{openproblems}
