\section{Cross-Cutting Gaps}\label{sec:synthesis}

Read side by side, the open problems of the six functions repeat themselves in different vocabularies.
A viewport predictor never tested in a closed loop, an offloading controller that never left the
simulator and an allocation scheme whose QoE objective was never shown to a user are three instances of
one shortfall. We condense the open problems of \cref{sec:encode,sec:anticipate,sec:compute,sec:provision,sec:connect,sec:assure}
into nine themes (\cref{tab:themes}) in three families: the \emph{evidence} that claims rest on, the
\emph{objectives} that designs optimise, and the \emph{blind spots} that most designs leave out.

\subsection{Evidence}
The first family concerns what claims are tested against. \emph{Shared data are missing} (T1). There are
no public traffic traces for AR offloading, avatar streams, twin-state updates, holographic calls or
haptic flows. No 360-degree dataset records head motion, network conditions and ratings in the same
session, and no latency benchmark couples motion, network delay and photodiode-measured MTP. Even simple
baselines are often absent. A persistence predictor reached a hit rate of 0.8355 against 0.8402 for a
sequence-to-sequence model on the same data \cite{W2963428739}. \emph{Evaluation stops at the
simulator} (T2). Whole technology lines, among them reconfigurable surfaces carrying XR, terahertz XR
links and metaverse resource markets, have never met hardware. Where learned controllers were tested on
unseen users or content, they degraded \cite{W4403826584,W4400409717}. \emph{Standards are not the
substrate} (T3). Standard XR traffic models appear almost only in radio simulations. When one was
checked against measurement, it underestimated cloud-VR bitrate by 25\% \cite{W4399340506}. The three
gaps share a cause. Traces and testbeds are costly to build, and the venues that publish algorithms
rarely credit them.

\subsection{Objectives}
The second family concerns what is optimised. \emph{Network metrics are not linked to experience} (T4).
Most objectives called ``QoE'' are formulas whose weights were chosen rather than fitted. Where users
were asked, their answers diverged from the proxies. Packet loss outweighed delay in VR cloud gaming
\cite{W4404916373}. Users of collaborative VR could not tell forms of latency apart \cite{W4392600587}.
CloudVR users did not notice 20~ms of added latency \cite{W2896076858}, while offloading designs optimise
to budgets of 5--13~ms. \emph{Requirements are inherited} (T5). Visions state 100--250~Mbps per metaverse
user \cite{W4313291084,W4312940001}, while a measured commercial social-VR platform used 2--3~Mbps
downstream \cite{W4294643390}. Holographic figures range from 5--100~Mbps for compressed calls
\cite{W4312662428} to 6.63~TBps \cite{W4300960574}. Haptic users tolerated up to 200~ms with adaptive
control \cite{W4200154132}, against the 1~ms figure that motivates much tactile-internet work. A
requirement that names neither the representation, the codec, the loop nor the percentile cannot be
checked.

\subsection{Blind spots}
The third family concerns what is left out. XR sends a continuous upstream, yet the \emph{uplink} (T6)
is studied far less than the downlink, and where it has been measured it is the bottleneck. On public
LTE, multi-user AR had a median end-to-end latency of 12.5~s, with the radio access network contributing
31.2\% \cite{W3047439371}. With three headsets on Wi-Fi~6, uplink OFDMA delayed tracking data
\cite{W4396853623}. \emph{Scale} (T7) is untested. Measured social platforms scale linearly because they
forward all avatar data \cite{W4307020344}, and controlled QoE studies use pairs of users. \emph{Security
is not costed} (T8). Attacks are measured and effective: keystrokes were inferred from avatar motion with
97.62\% accuracy \cite{W4398796475}. Defences, however, are seldom priced in frame latency.
\emph{Transport} (T9) has not followed the move from buffered streaming to interactive delivery. Users
rank loss above delay \cite{W3095344018,W4404916373}, yet forward error correction and L4S are rarely
applied. An L4S pipeline for volumetric conferencing converged 45\% faster than Google Congestion Control
\cite{W4408870658}, but only in a controlled bottleneck.

\subsection{How the gaps depend on each other}
The gaps are not independent (\cref{fig:themes}). Two are roots. Without open traces (T1), simulations
cannot be validated (T2), requirements cannot be derived (T5), standard traffic models cannot be checked
(T3), and uplink and multi-user workloads cannot even be described (T6, T7). Without a validated mapping
from network metrics to experience (T4), results on allocation, transport and security (T8, T9) cannot
be compared in units that matter to users. Two smaller questions point the same way. Device-energy
savings from offloading rest on models, and the few measurements disagree: one standalone headset
drained its battery 15\% faster when offloading wirelessly \cite{W4362489903}, while an edge VR testbed
found lower client power \cite{W4296339041}. Neural scene representations have no tested rate
adaptation, cache or split point. Both questions need the same two roots, and this ordering shapes the
agenda that follows.
