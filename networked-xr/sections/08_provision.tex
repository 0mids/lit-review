\section{Provisioning the Network: Radio, Resources, Transport and Architecture}\label{sec:provision}

XR traffic is unlike the traffic that networks were built for. It is \emph{periodic}: the renderer emits
a frame every 11--17~ms and the headset reports pose at least as often. It is \emph{bursty}: each frame
leaves the encoder as a block of hundreds of kilobytes that must cross the link before the next one
\cite{W2838572662}. It is \emph{bidirectional}: pose, input and, for edge AR, camera frames go up while
rendered content comes down. It is \emph{deadline-bound}: a frame that misses its display slot is worth
no more than a lost one. Best-effort networks assume elastic flows that value average rate; XR breaks
that assumption. Provisioning must therefore supply rate, delay and reliability per frame, not per flow,
and the tail of frame delivery, not its mean, decides whether the user sees a stall. Traffic volume
depends on where computation is split (\cref{sec:compute}): a remote renderer sends video, a local
renderer sends scene state. This section works bottom-up (\cref{fig:provision-arch}): link, scheduler,
transport and architecture.

\subsection{The link: a directional channel on a moving head}
The last hop must carry multi-gigabit bursts to a head that turns and is easily blocked, and its tail,
not its peak rate, is the limit. Four families address it.

\emph{Directional links} (mmWave, THz) buy rate with narrow beams and pay in fragility. THz analysis
gives 99.999\% reliability at 20~ms with guaranteed line of sight, but with blockage reliability falls
to 96\% and tail delays reach 30--90~ms while the mean stays below 20~ms \cite{W3022996137}. Commercial
hardware lost up to 4.72\% of packets under 45$^\circ$/s head rotation \cite{W4401507876}.

\emph{Diversity and pose-steered beams} are the remedy that holds. Dual-beam reception with coordinated
access points raised reliability by up to 22.8\% on real headset motion \cite{W4409076649}. Beams
steered from head pose reuse the motion predictors of \cref{sec:anticipate}, so prediction error bounds
their gain.

\emph{Reconfigurable surfaces} (RIS) promise to route around blockers, but the promise is assumed.
RIS-assisted XR has never run on hardware; learned RIS control violated a 20~ms bound beyond 15
simulated users \cite{W3207401133}, and optimisers on practical prototypes take minutes
\cite{W4401507876} where they must react within a head turn.

\emph{Shared medium access} binds on Wi-Fi and 5G before physical rate does. With frames aligned to the
802.11ad beacon interval, eight headsets at 400~Mbit/s met a 1~ms bound \cite{W3081368317}, but uplink
OFDMA with three headsets disrupted tracking on real hardware \cite{W4396853623}. On 5G-Advanced,
code-block-group retransmission with outer-loop link adaptation reports up to 67\% more satisfied XR
users \cite{W4315777456}, in system-level simulation only.

\subsection{The scheduler: deciding per frame who gets what}
A scheduler divides spectrum, power, edge GPU time and cache among users at every slot. These resources
substitute: cached fields of view and on-device computing can stand in for bandwidth \cite{W2887223659}.
The tension is between the model a scheduler optimises and the traffic it meets;
\cref{tab:provision-families} groups four families.

\emph{Optimisation} wins when its models hold: multicasting overlapping 360-degree viewports over mmWave
under a Lyapunov delay constraint raised HD delivery by 40--50\% and cut 99th-percentile delay by
17--37\% against unicast, in simulation driven by 50 real head traces \cite{W2901851646}.
\emph{Heuristics} trade guarantees for deployability: a collaborative-VR allocator reached at least half
the optimum and ran on commodity phones \cite{W4312555471}. \emph{Learning} schedulers beat greedy,
random and proportional-fair baselines \cite{W2596760110}, but these baselines are weak, and learning
and optimisation are rarely compared on the same traces. On an O-RAN testbed, latency-estimation control
met 10--16.67~ms targets but over-provisioned, while packet-size prediction was efficient but lost
latency control \cite{W4410491869}.

\emph{XR-aware mechanisms} change what the radio sees rather than how it optimises. A PDU set lets the
RAN drop or prioritise a frame's packets together under one delay budget; a delay-tracking PDU-set
scheduler served 4--5 times more satisfied users than earlier PDU-set-aware schedulers under scarce
resources \cite{W7168254691}. Discontinuous reception aligned to the frame period saves headset power
\cite{W3209239392}, and configured grants could pre-allocate the periodic pose uplink, but no testbed
has compared them with dynamic grants.

Three problems sit above the family choice. \emph{Joint radio--rendering allocation} treats GPU capacity
as a free variable \cite{W4379382423}; only one testbed adapted base-station bandwidth and edge GPU
frequency together \cite{W4413321019}. \emph{Multicast} gains depend on viewport overlap
\cite{W2901851646}, so they are unproven for interactive 6DoF content. \emph{The objective} is
contested: schedulers optimise a utility of tracking and transmission delay \cite{W2596760110}, an
attention-weighted ``Meta-Immersion'' score \cite{W4379382423} or weighted sums of quality and delay
\cite{W4312555471}. Each reports gains of 20--37\% in its own metric, none validated with users
(\cref{sec:assure}). Traffic inputs are optimistic too: the 3GPP baseline model underestimated
effective VR bitrate by 25\% against a measured headset \cite{W4399340506}.

\subsection{Transport: congestion and loss for interactive frames}
End to end, frames cross shared bottlenecks where loss-based congestion control fills queues, and a
retransmission usually arrives after the frame's deadline. Users find loss the greater threat: in cloud
VR, RTT up to 90~ms was hardly noticed while loss up to 8\% mattered \cite{W3095344018}. Stalls of up
to four frames at 90~fps went unnoticed, and reprojection helped from eight frames upward
\cite{W4301184864}, a margin that the compute pipeline sets (\cref{sec:compute}). Three families
address the problem.

\emph{Low-queue congestion control} keeps the bottleneck short. An L4S pipeline for volumetric
conferencing converged 45\% faster than Google congestion control and lost no frames where WebRTC lost
3\% \cite{W4408870658}, but on a controlled bottleneck with one competing scheme; radio links with fast
capacity swings are untested. \emph{Session protocols} decide how media and control share a connection:
Media over QUIC, RTP over QUIC and WebRTC have been compared for holographic conferencing over Wi-Fi and
5G \cite{W4416047836}, and aligning frame delivery with the display's VSync removed up to 14.3\% of
frame misses \cite{W2838572662}. \emph{Loss protection} repairs damage without waiting for a round
trip, yet forward error correction for remote rendering has barely been studied \cite{W4409129991},
although users weigh loss most.

\subsection{Architecture: where functions sit and what the network must promise}
Architectures decide which tier hosts rendering, state and caching, and what the network must deliver.
Four families recur. \emph{Edge-centric} designs make edge computing a necessary tier
\cite{W4312940001}. \emph{Sliced} designs isolate XR traffic and have testbed evidence: AR, cloud gaming
and video calls were measured under RAN-slice configurations on an O-RAN 5G network \cite{W4386887692}.
\emph{6G and metaverse visions} add THz access, AI-native control and ledgers \cite{W4224330100}, but
rarely map their functions onto the 5G-Advanced XR mechanisms that already exist, so what 6G must add
remains unstated. \emph{Measured platforms} tell a plainer story: throughput, latency and device load on
five social VR platforms grew almost linearly with users because servers forward avatar data directly
\cite{W4307020344}. The scaling bottleneck there is server logic, not access capacity
(\cref{sec:connect}).

Requirements need the same discipline as results. Stated per-user rates exceed measured ones by two to
five orders of magnitude (\cref{tab:provision-requirements}) because the operating point is left
unstated: an uncompressed light field is not a compressed point cloud. A requirement means something
only with its representation, codec, split point and control loop attached. The figures also propagate:
one survey reproduces another's requirement table with a reliability target changed from $1-10^{-6}$ to
$1-10^{-5}$ \cite{W4390204075,W4312940001}, so the apparent consensus is partly one source counted
several times. Ledgers, finally, are placed in the interactive data path for ownership and trust
\cite{W4224330100,W4312940001}, yet no study has measured the delay they add.

\begin{openproblems}
\item \textbf{Which objective should an XR scheduler optimise?} Allocators optimise incompatible QoE
  constructs that no user study has validated. A QoE-in-the-loop slicing study on an open 5G testbed,
  comparing static, latency-target and QoE-model xApps with ratings and sickness scores, would decide
  which objective matters.
\item \textbf{Do joint radio--rendering allocators survive measured costs?} Rendering capacity is a
  free variable in these models. Profiling render and encode time and GPU power on edge hardware, then
  re-running published allocators under measured costs, would show whether their rankings hold.
\item \textbf{Can RIS or THz links meet frame deadlines for real XR traffic?} RIS work is simulated and
  THz reliability rests on Poisson traffic. A pose-driven RIS prototype at 28 or 60~GHz, measured on frame
  deadline misses under head motion, would test the premise.
\item \textbf{How should the uplink be granted?} Pose and camera uplink enter motion-to-photon latency
  directly, yet configured, dynamic and pose-prioritised grants have never been compared on a testbed
  with tracking error as the outcome.
\item \textbf{How should congestion control and loss protection share a frame deadline?} Users weigh
  loss above delay, but L4S and forward error correction are tested only on emulated bottlenecks.
  Comparing them over cellular and Wi-Fi with cross traffic, reporting 99th-percentile frame latency,
  would settle the design.
\item \textbf{What do shared worlds actually require?} Requirement tables are copied, not derived.
  Traffic measurements of shared-world platforms across user counts and avatar fidelities, compared with
  standard XR traffic models, would replace them.
\end{openproblems}
