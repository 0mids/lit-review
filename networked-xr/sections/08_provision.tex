\section{Provisioning the Network: Radio, Resources, Transport and Architecture}\label{sec:provision}

This section asks how access networks, schedulers, transport protocols and system architectures supply
the rate, delay and reliability that XR needs. Provisioning is the fastest-growing function in the
corpus. It holds \fnProvisionN{} papers (\fnProvisionSh{}), and its share of output rose from
\fnProvisionEarly{} in 2017--19 to \fnProvisionLate{} in 2022--24, while the share of papers built on 6G
rose from \nxrShTechSixGBeyondFiveGEarly{} to \nxrShTechSixGBeyondFiveGLate{}. Three cells hold the work:
resource allocation (\nxrNProbResourceAllocation{} papers), architecture and vision
(\nxrNProbArchitectureVision{}) and wireless link design (\nxrNProbWirelessLinkDesign{}). They differ
sharply in evidence. \nxrSimResourceAllocation{} of allocation papers are simulations and
\nxrRealResourceAllocation{} use a real testbed; \nxrNoEvalArchitectureVision{} of architecture papers
have no evaluation at all; link design is the most empirical (\nxrRealWirelessLinkDesign{} real
testbeds). No paper in any of the three cells asked users what they perceived. The argument of this
section is that provisioning research has solved well-posed optimisation problems whose inputs (QoE
functions, rendering costs, traffic and requirement figures) were assumed rather than measured.
\Cref{fig:provision-arch} places each strand's decisions in the delivery chain; offloading decisions
are treated in \cref{sec:compute}.

\subsection{Allocating radio, compute and money}
Allocation divides spectrum, power, edge GPU time and caches among XR users so that each meets a delay
or quality target. The established result is that these resources substitute for each other. Caching
rendered fields of view and computing on the device can stand in for bandwidth
\cite{W2887223659,W2945507395}, and joint uplink, downlink and edge allocation saves mobile energy in
collaborative AR \cite{W2608841173}. Multicasting overlapping 360-degree viewports over mmWave raised
HD delivery by 40--50\% and cut 99th-percentile delay by 17--37\% against unicast, in simulation driven
by 50 real head traces \cite{W2901851646}; the gain shrinks as viewports diverge, so it is unproven for
interactive 6DoF content.

The objective is contested. The cell optimises at least four incompatible QoE constructs: a utility of
tracking, processing and transmission delay \cite{W2596760110}, regressions of SSIM and VMAF
\cite{W3214250514}, an attention-weighted ``Meta-Immersion'' score \cite{W4379382423} and weighted sums
of quality, delay and variation \cite{W4312555471}. Gains of 20--37\% are reported in each paper's own
metric and cannot be compared, and none of the \nxrSynXRAQoEN{} allocation papers that optimise QoE was
evaluated with users. Method is disputed too. Reinforcement-learning schedulers beat greedy, random and
proportional-fair baselines \cite{W2596760110,W4381946597}, while optimisation papers reach
near-optimal decisions four orders of magnitude faster than a commercial solver \cite{W4324125331}; the
two camps rarely meet on the same traces.

Much of the rest is assumed. Rendering capacity is a free variable in joint radio--rendering allocation
\cite{W4379382423}; the only measured GPU figure is economic, a 4~GB vGPU slice that halved cloud-XR
hourly cost while holding MOS at 4.38 or above \cite{W7115818680}. Metaverse markets are the extreme
case: a learned double Dutch auction reached social welfare within about 5\% of the optimum
\cite{W3214250514}, but every auction and incentive scheme in the cell uses synthetic valuations and
arrivals. Standards-aligned work is more realistic about traffic and schedules PDU sets \cite{W4410582180}. A
delay-tracking PDU-set scheduler served 4--5 times more satisfied users than earlier PDU-set-aware
schedulers under scarce resources \cite{W7168254691}, yet only \nxrSynXRARLStdN{} of the \nxrSynXRARLN{} reinforcement-learning
allocators use 3GPP XR models. Real traffic undermines static provisioning: VR frame sizes fluctuate
even under constant-bitrate encoding \cite{W4379468406}. On O-RAN testbeds, latency-estimation control
met 10--16.67~ms targets but over-provisioned \cite{W4410491869}, and a latency-steering xApp freed
15.8\% more resources than an equivalent static slice \cite{W4392175446}.

\subsection{The last hop: directional links, Wi-Fi and 5G}
Link design must keep a multi-gigabit, low-delay channel to a moving head, and the tail, not the peak
rate, is the limit. THz analysis gives 99.999\% reliability at 20~ms with guaranteed line of sight, but
under blockage reliability falls to 96\% at 20~ms and tail delays reach 30--90~ms while the mean stays
below 20~ms \cite{W3022996137}. At 60~GHz, 3D head motion defeated beam search designed for 2D
\cite{W2791171246}, and commercial hardware lost up to 4.72\% of packets under 45$^\circ$/s rotation
\cite{W4401507876}. Spatial diversity is the remedy that holds across studies. Dual-beam reception with
coordinated access points raised reliability by up to 22.8\% on real headset motion \cite{W4409076649},
distributed antennas kept 12~Gb/s under full rotation \cite{W4413277491}, and steering beams from head pose helps \cite{W4313170175}: predictive beams from a neural radio
field halved beam-search error on a 28~GHz array \cite{W7204893690}.

On Wi-Fi and 5G the bound is medium access, not physical rate. With frames aligned to the 802.11ad
beacon interval, eight headsets at 400~Mbit/s met a 1~ms bound \cite{W3081368317}. Wi-Fi~7 multi-link
operation served 7 rather than 6 AR stations at 80~MHz \cite{W4376480829}. Whether downlink or uplink
binds depends on the assumed rates \cite{W4376480829,W4409221564}; on real hardware, uplink OFDMA with
three headsets disrupted tracking \cite{W4396853623}. 5G-Advanced link adaptation reports up to 67\%
more satisfied XR users \cite{W4315777456}, entirely in system-level simulation. Two technologies rest
on assumption alone. RIS-assisted XR has never run on hardware, and RIS optimisers that take seconds per
slot \cite{W4318586163} must react within a head rotation \cite{W3207401133}. No XR traffic has crossed
a THz link; the analyses assume Poisson arrivals and fixed-size frames \cite{W3022996137}.

\subsection{Transport over shared bottlenecks}
Transport decides what reaches the headset when the bottleneck is shared and lossy. Its share of the
corpus fell from \nxrShTechInternetTransportWebEarly{} to \nxrShTechInternetTransportWebLate{} as
delivery moved from buffered streaming to interactive rendering, although interactive traffic absorbs
loss less well. Users confirm the risk. In cloud VR, RTT up to 90~ms was hardly noticed while loss of up
to 8\% mattered \cite{W3095344018}; with CloudXR, QoE fell at RTT of 75~ms or loss above 6\% (N=30)
\cite{W4402156718}; with Air Link, loss raised cybersickness more than delay did (N=16)
\cite{W4404916373}. Yet only \nxrSynXRendFECN{} remote-rendering papers address forward error
correction or error resilience \cite{W4399340506,W4409129991}.

Congestion control for XR is nascent. L4S appears in \nxrSynXLFourSN{} corpus papers. An L4S pipeline
for volumetric conferencing converged 45\% faster than Google congestion control and lost no frames
where WebRTC lost 3\% \cite{W4408870658}, and L4S has been carried over 5G for split rendering
\cite{W4401507827}; both used controlled bottlenecks with one competing scheme. Practice is ahead of
research: Steam Link carries video and control in one QUIC tunnel \cite{W7160626692}, and WebRTC
selective forwarding adapted 13--36\% faster than TCP for volumetric streams in emulation
\cite{W7151958829}.

\subsection{Architectures and the figures they carry}
Architecture papers decide where functions sit and state what the network must deliver. They agree on
the qualitative list (high rate, low delay, reliability, interoperability) and on edge computing as a
necessary tier \cite{W4312940001,W4315497913,W4391305431}. The quantitative figures are where they fail.
\Cref{tab:provision-requirements} sets stated requirements against measured ones; for rates the gap
spans two to five orders of magnitude. The cause is unstated operating points: an uncompressed light
field is not a compressed point cloud, and 360-degree video is not avatar state.

The figures propagate. One survey reproduces another's requirement table with a reliability target
changed from $1-10^{-6}$ to $1-10^{-5}$ \cite{W4390204075,W4312940001}, and its research agenda
reproduces a third \cite{W4224330100}; the apparent consensus is partly one source counted several
times. Measured platforms tell a different story: throughput, latency and device load on five social VR
platforms grew almost linearly with users, because servers forward avatar data directly
\cite{W4307020344,W4294643390}. The scaling bottleneck is server logic, not access capacity.

Two architectural claims remain untested. Blockchain is placed in the interactive data path for
ownership and trust \cite{W4224330100,W4312940001}; \nxrSynXArchChainN{} architecture papers include a
ledger, \nxrSynXArchChainNoEval{} of them without evaluation, and none has measured the delay it adds,
although one concedes that replication raises delay with users \cite{W4320717855}. Network slicing for
XR, by contrast, has testbed evidence: AR, cloud gaming and video calls were measured under RAN-slice
configurations on an O-RAN 5G network \cite{W4386887692}. 6G visions rarely map their functions onto
the 5G-Advanced XR mechanisms in \cref{fig:provision-arch} \cite{W4387933382,W4376607343}, so what 6G
must add remains unstated.

\begin{openproblems}
\item \textbf{Allocation objectives are not validated with users.} None of the \nxrSynXRAQoEN{}
  QoE-driven allocators was evaluated with participants. A QoE-in-the-loop slicing study on an open 5G
  testbed, comparing static, latency-target and QoE-model xApps with ratings and sickness scores, would
  show which objective matters.
\item \textbf{Rendering costs in joint radio--GPU allocation are assumed.} Profiling render and encode
  time and GPU power on edge hardware, then re-running published allocators under measured costs, would
  show whether their rankings survive.
\item \textbf{RIS and THz have never carried XR traffic.} All RIS-for-XR work is simulated, and THz
  reliability rests on Poisson traffic. A pose-driven RIS prototype at 28 or 60~GHz, measured on frame
  deadline misses, would test the premise.
\item \textbf{Requirement figures are not derived from measured traffic.} Stated per-user rates exceed
  measured ones by up to two orders of magnitude. A traffic atlas of shared-world platforms across user
  counts and avatar fidelities, compared with 3GPP models, would replace copied tables.
\item \textbf{Transport and loss resilience are untested on real bottlenecks.} Users weigh loss above
  delay, yet L4S appears in \nxrSynXLFourSN{} papers. Comparing L4S, delay-based and loss-based control
  with forward error correction over cellular and Wi-Fi links with cross traffic, reporting p99 frame
  latency, would fill this.
\item \textbf{Blockchain latency is uncosted.} No study measures ledger delay against interaction
  deadlines. A benchmark of centralised, permissioned and rollup ledgers inside a live session would
  settle whether the ledger belongs in the data path.
\end{openproblems}
