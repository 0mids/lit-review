\section{Delivering Ahead of the User: Prediction, Adaptation and Caching}\label{sec:anticipate}

An immersive client displays only a fraction of what it could receive, and it must request that fraction before the user looks there. Delivery is therefore a bet on the user's future attention. Three decisions place the bet: \emph{what to predict} about the user, \emph{what to fetch} given that prediction and the network, and \emph{where to keep} content close to the user. They form one loop (\cref{fig:anticipate-arch}) in which the prediction horizon, the buffer depth and the cost of a wrong commitment constrain one another; \cref{tab:anticipate-families} classifies the approaches. Tiling belongs to \cref{sec:encode}, rendering delay to \cref{sec:compute}, and transport to \cref{sec:provision}.

\subsection{What to predict: horizon against accuracy}

Prediction trades horizon for accuracy. The target is head orientation for 3DoF video and the full 6DoF pose for volumetric content, where position and occlusion change what is visible. Accuracy falls steeply with horizon. On a 130-user trace set, Flare's best predictor reached median accuracies of 90.5\%, 72.9\%, 58.2\% and 35.2\% at 0.2, 0.5, 1 and 3~s \cite{W2896840651}, and a cross-dataset benchmark found no algorithm accurate much beyond 300~ms \cite{W3089527252}, whereas streaming buffers hold seconds of content.

Three families of predictors address this gap. \emph{Extrapolators} (persistence, linear regression, Kalman filters) assume smooth motion over the horizon and need no training. \emph{Learned sequence models} (LSTM, GRU, transformer) learn from the user's past trajectory. \emph{Content-aware and cross-user predictors} add what a trajectory cannot know: saliency \cite{W2624991012}, gaze, or earlier viewers' paths, the strongest long-horizon signal for on-demand content. Cross-user prediction raised accuracy by 20.2 percentage points \cite{W2896449077}, and other users' paths helped most at 4--10~s \cite{W2963428739}. Live and interactive content lack this history, so these results do not transfer to them.

Whether learned models beat simple ones is contested. On the Tsinghua dataset, persistence reached a hit rate of 0.8355, against 0.8402 for sequence-to-sequence and 0.8464 for LSTM models \cite{W2963428739}; for 6DoF pose, simple statistical models performed comparably \cite{W4379805466}. Learned models report large gains mostly over each other: a fusion transformer's average precision of 0.954 at 1~s, against 0.901 for the previous best, came from a random split that shared videos and users between training and test \cite{W4387148495}. Without extrapolation baselines and video- and user-disjoint splits, accuracy cannot be separated from leakage. A second assumption is that a point estimate suffices, although the fetcher needs a distribution to size its margin; predicting the predictor's own deviation saved 45\% of bandwidth at under 0.1\% failure \cite{W2585813514}, yet calibration of predicted probabilities is rarely reported.

\subsection{What to fetch: turning probabilities into requests}

The fetcher turns a prediction and a throughput estimate into requests for spatial units, qualities and segments. Its objective, a hand-weighted sum of viewport quality, rebuffering and switches, is assumed rather than measured. Hedging against prediction error is the common core: 360ProbDASH fetched tiles in proportion to their viewing probability and raised viewport PSNR by about 39\% \cite{W2766324256}, and most designs add low-quality margin tiles.

The controllers fall into three families. \emph{Heuristic, buffer-based controllers} map buffer level and view probability to quality with fixed rules; BOLA360 adds a near-optimality proof without training and reports 6--110\% QoE gains \cite{W4394890851}. \emph{Optimisation-based controllers} solve a rate-utility allocation, often a knapsack over tiles or 3D cells \cite{W2963389850}; they are exact for their model and fragile when its inputs are wrong. \emph{Learned controllers}, mostly deep reinforcement learning, train on trace replay and report 20--30\% gains over heuristics \cite{W2920378308}, but degrade on unseen viewing patterns and networks \cite{W4403826584}. No study has compared buffer-based and learned controllers on shared traces under one objective.

Six degrees of freedom change the unit of adaptation from a tile to a 3D cell, an object or a level of detail (LoD). ViVo combined viewport, distance and occlusion visibility and saved 40\% of data on average at SSIM above 0.99 \cite{W3021314748}. No general allocation policy has emerged: the best multi-object strategy depends on the scene and the camera path \cite{W2982262421}.

Live delivery removes the pre-encoded quality ladder and the cross-user history, and two-way volumetric communication breaks the buffer model altogether: a tiled user-adaptive point-cloud system needed 14~Mbps instead of about 300~Mbps in a 33-user study \cite{W4304091566}. In such systems adaptation is coupled loosely, if at all, to congestion control (\cref{sec:provision}).

\subsection{Where to keep it: buffers, caches and shared delivery}

Keeping content close buys the right to be wrong cheaply, and it pays most when viewers' attention overlaps. \emph{Popularity caches} (LRU, LFU) treat tiles as independent objects and are what deployed HTTP caches run. \emph{Viewport-aware caches and prefetchers} place tiles by cross-user viewing: viewport-similarity placement raised the edge hit ratio by at least 40\% over LFU and 17\% over LRU \cite{W2896595273}, and after 24 views OpCASH served over 95\% of the viewport from cache while fetching 85\% less from the origin \cite{W4288370107}. Independent work replicates the gain \cite{W3021782077}, but only against popularity baselines, never an oracle such as Belady's. \emph{Shared delivery} exploits overlap on the air rather than in storage: multicast of similar viewports saved over 50\% \cite{W2725312916}, and coded caching promises gains that grow with the number of users, although subpacketisation and synchronised requests may erase them \cite{W4381251523}.

Three questions are open. Cache hits distort the throughput estimates of DASH clients \cite{W3122351391}, yet most caching designs assume an ideal client. Whether learned policies add value cannot be settled while XRFlux remains the only shared benchmark \cite{W4409763640}. That hit ratio stands for experience is assumed: no XR caching scheme has been evaluated with viewers. Caching of 6DoF content remains almost unstudied.

\subsection{One loop: horizon, misprediction and realistic savings}

The three decisions share one variable, the time between commitment and display. A deep buffer absorbs throughput dips but commits bits on predictions seconds ahead; a shallow one needs short predictions but stalls on dips. Buffer length is therefore prediction horizon. Short segments of at most 2--3~s ease the tension \cite{W2527384707}, and upgrading buffered tiles lets the fetcher revise a bet as the prediction sharpens. An edge cache moves the commitment closer to display, so caching and prediction are complements, not substitutes. Remote rendering faces the same horizon, with pose prediction hiding the round trip rather than throughput dips (\cref{sec:compute}).

A wrong bet is paid in one of two currencies: a thin margin shows low-quality or blank tiles, and a wide margin spends the saving. Tiling adds a fixed cost: a median coding overhead of 7.3--10.4\% for 1-s tiles and a rise in Flare's phone CPU load from 18\% to 58\% \cite{W2896840651}, and up to 37.3\% overhead for an 8$\times$5 grid \cite{W2623784065} (\cref{sec:encode}).

The net saving falls as the evaluation becomes real. Perfect knowledge of future motion bounds it at 80\% \cite{W2585813514}. HEVC tiles saved 65\% with a static viewport and over 40\% with recorded head motion \cite{W2623784065}; Flare on phones over commercial LTE, with four users and three videos, saved 26--35\% \cite{W2896840651}. The gradient recurs in 6DoF: ViVo over commercial 5G saved 40\% for headset users but only 10\% for phone users watching the same content, because people move differently on each device \cite{W3021314748}.

It is established that viewport-adaptive delivery saves a substantial share of bytes; how large that share is on wireless headsets with real viewers, and whether viewers notice it, is not. Most evidence comes from one on-demand client on an emulated link, only two used commercial cellular traces \cite{W3021314748,W2810704880}, and mainstream quality models failed on a database of 378 viewport-adaptive videos \cite{W4402916896}. No user study has placed several headsets behind one bottleneck, the normal case at home or in a venue.

\begin{openproblems}
\item \textbf{Do predictor rankings survive in closed loop?} Persistence nearly matches learned predictors offline, and accuracy is rarely tied to streaming outcome. Running extrapolators and learned predictors under one controller, over real bandwidth, with disjoint splits and a viewer rating study, would show whether accuracy gains reach viewers.
\item \textbf{Can calibrated uncertainty replace fixed margins?} Controllers need view probabilities but receive point estimates. Distribution-free calibration wrapped around any predictor, with margins chosen for a target miss rate, would show how much bandwidth honest uncertainty saves.
\item \textbf{What objective should the fetcher maximise?} Weights of quality, stalls and switches are chosen by hand, and quality models fail on viewport-adaptive streams. Fitting the objective to user ratings, or adopting tile extensions of standard models \cite{W3200608537,W3168206114}, would make controller rankings meaningful.
\item \textbf{How should adaptation behave over real wireless access and shared bottlenecks?} Paired head-motion and radio traces from Wi-Fi and commercial 5G, replayed and run live with several headsets on one access point, would test whether radio hints and access-point coordination reduce stalls and unfairness.
\item \textbf{What should an edge keep for 6DoF content, and do cache gains reach viewers?} A level-of-detail cache shared by co-located users, and an HTTP edge serving tiled DASH under popularity, viewport-aware and Belady policies with viewers, would show whether hit-ratio gains are perceptible.
\end{openproblems}
