\section{What Is Sent: Representing and Encoding Immersive Content}\label{sec:encode}

Every networked XR system first decides what its content is: a
sphere of pixels, a set of points, a bundle of camera views, the parameters of a neural scene model or a
stream of force samples. This section asks which representation travels over the network and how it is
compressed. The coding cell holds \fnEncodeN{} papers (\fnEncodeSh{} of the corpus); its share rose from
\fnEncodeEarly{} in 2017--2019 to \fnEncodeLate{} in 2022--2024. The content has shifted: the
share of 360-degree video papers fell from \nxrShContVideoThreeSixtyEarly{} to
\nxrShContVideoThreeSixtyLate{}, that of volumetric content rose from
\nxrShContVolumetricPointcloudEarly{} to \nxrShContVolumetricPointcloudLate{}, and Gaussian splatting
(\nxrKwGaussianSplattingEarly{} papers, then \nxrKwGaussianSplattingLate{}) and semantic communication
(\nxrKwSemanticCommunicationEarly{}, then \nxrKwSemanticCommunicationLate{}) appeared from nothing.
Evaluation lags: \fnEncodeSimAna{} of coding papers are judged by simulation or analysis,
\fnEncodeNone{} not at all and \fnEncodeUsers{} with users. The argument of this section is that the
representation fixes three quantities before any network algorithm acts (the bitrate floor, the
encoding delay and the decoding cost on the device), and that new representations are proposed faster
than these quantities are measured. \Cref{fig:encode-chains} compares the chains. Adaptation and
caching are treated in \cref{sec:anticipate}, haptic transport and holoportation in \cref{sec:connect}.

\subsection{360-degree video: a coding problem on a sphere}
360-degree video reuses the 2D video chain; its questions sit at the two ends, in how the sphere is
mapped to rectangles and how many decoders the client can afford. The established result is that
concentrating bits on the viewport saves bitrate, but less than it saves pixels. Oculus's offset cube
used fewer than half the pixels of an equirectangular frame for similar view quality, yet saved only
5.6--16.4\% of bitrate, and switching among its 88 versions made the player download over 57\% extra segments \cite{W2623027537}. At a fixed 6~Mbps a cube map with a
full-quality front face beat three other layouts, and pyramids failed under head motion
\cite{W2527384707}.

The delivery unit is contested. Viewport-specific versions need no reassembly but multiply storage;
tiles share one encode but cost 7.3--10.4\% in coding efficiency \cite{W2896840651}, and up to 37.3\%
for small tiles at low resolution \cite{W2623784065}. Point-of-view scalable coding claims about 75\%
saving \cite{W2894699932}, against Oculus's 5.6--16.4\%; different baselines explain most of the gap. The client pays for tiles: Flare raised phone
CPU load from 18\% to 58\% \cite{W2896840651}, and streaming took 28.5\% of headset power
\cite{W2626466913}, both on 2017--2018 hardware. OMAF standardises projection, region-wise packing and
quality descriptors \cite{W3137710672}, yet network studies almost always assume tiled equirectangular
video, so their results may not transfer to deployed formats. Gaze adds a lever with user evidence:
gaze-based quantisation saved 40--47\% in cloud VR gaming \cite{W3174397927}.

\subsection{Volumetric content: points, meshes and views}
Volumetric content is one to three orders of magnitude heavier than 2D video, and its codecs trade
compression ratio against speed. Raw point clouds need 720~Mbit/s at 200K points and 30~fps
\cite{W3021314748}, and about 5~Gbit/s at one million points \cite{W4362496249}. V-PCC projects patches
onto planes and reuses video codecs; it reaches lossy ratios of 100--500 but took about 4~hours to encode
30 frames of one object \cite{W3096788322}. G-PCC and Draco code geometry in 3D, run fast and compress less, so live systems avoid V-PCC. VR2Gather's MPEG Anchor codec cut 281.8~Mbit/s to 19.0~Mbit/s
but raised latency from 40.6 to 278.7~ms, and parallel tiles brought it back to 90.6~ms
\cite{W4362496249}: coding, not transit, set the delay. Tuning the standard codecs yields single-digit
gains, such as 2--6\% BD-rate from V-PCC rate control \cite{W4405304599}; larger gains come from changing the representation, as when 3D patch coding matched
V-PCC's ratio with real-time decoding \cite{W4379928158}. The receiver is the second constraint: Hermes reached 30~fps on phones only by exploiting inter-frame correlation \cite{W4387967921}. A measurement of Draco, G-PCC, V-PCC and two neural codecs found that the best codec depends on content
and viewing behaviour \cite{W4321488303}.

What deployed live systems send is often views rather than geometry. Project Starline sends four colour
and three depth H.265 streams at 30--100~Mbit/s and fuses them at the receiver, because real-time mesh
or point-cloud compression was not possible at its resolution \cite{W4200107448}; MetaStream codes RGB-D
in 2D on the cameras and builds point clouds at the edge \cite{W4387212733}. MPEG Immersive Video (MIV)
standardises this multi-view-plus-depth path, with atlas packing \cite{W4210296418} and view synthesis at
the decoder \cite{W3034756801}, and reuses hardware video decoders. Yet only one of
about twenty corpus papers on MIV uses a real network, and none has users; light-field delivery has
been tested only offline \cite{W2902432774}. Whether to send geometry or views is open: no study compares point clouds, meshes, multi-view video and Gaussian
splats at matched perceived quality, bitrate and latency.

\subsection{Neural and semantic representations}
Neural representations replace explicit geometry with model parameters, and semantic codecs replace
pixels with meaning. Both promise large gains; neither has yet faced a variable network. The most
deployable design reuses existing hardware: V$^3$ bakes dynamic Gaussians into 2D videos for H.264
decoders and reaches 32.97~dB PSNR at 0.532~MB per frame and 27~fps on a phone \cite{W4404525293}. Training takes about a minute per frame on a desktop GPU, which rules
out live content, and download was measured only on an internal network. Other designs offer
single-model variable bitrate for NeRF video \cite{W4400667199} and full-scene splat streaming at 30~fps
on phones \cite{W4416477288}, but only two of some thirty neural coding papers address progressive
coding or bandwidth variation, and a doctoral plan still lists progressive transmission, quality
assessment and error control as open \cite{W4408847683}.

Semantic codecs report the largest savings and the weakest comparisons. WiserVR sent 1.81~Mbit instead
of 28.3~Mbit for 20 frames, in an AWGN simulation that excluded the delay of sharing the knowledge base
\cite{W4324296656}, and MagicStream reports up to 1195$\times$ less bandwidth \cite{W4404034698}. The
one generative prototype timed end to end cut bandwidth by 99.93\% but raised latency from 980~ms to
13.66~s \cite{W4403677254}. Fidelity is defined differently in each paper (PSNR on a noisy channel,
joint-position error, text similarity), task metrics can disagree with perceived realism
\cite{W4385452934}, and the baseline is usually BPG with LDPC rather than HEVC or VVC at matched quality.
An order-of-magnitude saving is plausible; its price in inference delay and device energy is assumed
rather than measured.

\subsection{Holograms and touch as signals}
Holographic and haptic content sit at opposite ends of the rate scale, and for both the requirement
figures depend on an unstated representation. Akyildiz and Guo define a hologram as a recorded light
field, then adopt point clouds as the working form \cite{W4300960574}. Their worked example, a
1.5~m$\times$0.75~m light-field display with 0.5~mm hogels, needs 221~GB per frame, or 6.63~TBps at
30~fps. Measured systems used 5--100~Mbps for compressed point clouds over live 5G \cite{W4312662428}
and 173~Mbps for full-duplex light-field telepresence \cite{W3176472668}. The gap of five to seven orders of magnitude separates
uncompressed display arithmetic from compressed capture; the two figures do not estimate one quantity.
For live use, codec delay binds first: V-PCC needs 11--42 minutes to encode one second
\cite{W4309030584}, and an uncompressed prototype ran at 8~fps with more than 300~ms of latency
\cite{W4395097214}. Optical holograms are only now becoming coded media, with a learnable JPEG-style
codec that decodes in 4.2~ms but is judged by PSNR alone \cite{W4417448513}.

Haptic signals need little rate and strict timing. Kinaesthetic samples are sent at 1~kHz or more to
keep the control loop stable \cite{W2912555763}. Perceptual deadband coding sends a sample only when its change exceeds a
Weber fraction (7--15\% for force) and removes 80--90\% of packets without perceptible loss; two
independent implementations of the IEEE~1918.1.1 kinaesthetic codec reproduced the same rates and errors
\cite{W2912555763}. The standard separates this delay-free coding from vibrotactile coding, which may
use block transforms with tens of milliseconds of algorithmic delay \cite{W2911218692}. These results
hold for a single contact point. Learned multichannel codecs report cuts of 80--92\%
\cite{W7131889935,W4406137775}, mostly in simulation and against each other rather than a standard
anchor; the one standard-compliant encoder in the corpus still calls the standard future work
\cite{W4410887405}. Deadband coding also makes traffic bursty, so the periodic 1~ms flow that transport
studies assume is not what a codec emits.

\begin{openproblems}
\item \textbf{Semantic and generative codecs are not evaluated end to end.} Of the semantic papers in the
  cell, one uses a real testbed and none reports a subjective test. Comparing them with HEVC, VVC and
  V-PCC at matched rated quality on an open 5G testbed, timing encoder and decoder inference, would
  show whether bandwidth savings survive latency.
\item \textbf{New representations have no perceptual ground truth.} No public rating dataset exists for
  compressed Gaussian, neural or generative reconstructions viewed in a headset, so PSNR and LPIPS are
  unvalidated for their artefacts. A six-degrees-of-freedom rating study across codecs and rates would
  supply one.
\item \textbf{Geometry and views have never been compared, and MIV is almost untested on networks.}
  One of about twenty MIV papers uses a real network. Encoding the same scenes as point clouds, meshes,
  MIV and Gaussian splats over replayed 5G traces, at matched rated quality, would show which
  representation minimises bitrate and latency.
\item \textbf{Codecs are tuned at fixed rates.} Coding papers rarely use bandwidth traces, cellular
  links or 3GPP XR traffic models, and neural codecs lack bitrate ladders. Rate control driven by
  measured capacity, evaluated on frame-deadline misses, would test whether codec gains hold on mobile
  links.
\item \textbf{Hologram requirements describe no real hologram.} Half the holographic papers name no
  representation, and no measured traffic trace exists for any of them. A benchmark that captures one
  scene, codes it as point clouds, splats, light fields and phase holograms, and records traces, would
  replace requirement tables with measured figures.
\item \textbf{Coded haptic streams lack shared content and loss tests with users.} Haptic codecs are
  evaluated on one-off recordings, and none under realistic loss with participants. Released reference
  recordings and a within-subjects study of deadband and vibrotactile codecs over replayed 5G and Wi-Fi
  impairments would close both gaps.
\end{openproblems}
