\section{What Is Sent: Representing and Encoding Immersive Content}\label{sec:encode}

Every networked XR system must first decide what its content is: a sphere of pixels, a set of camera
views, a cloud of points, the parameters of a learned scene model or a stream of force samples. The
design question is which representation travels and how it is compressed. The choice is
made before any network algorithm acts, and it fixes four quantities that every later function inherits.
The \emph{bitrate floor} is the rate below which quality collapses, and it sets what the network must
supply (\cref{sec:provision}). The \emph{encoder delay} is spent before the first bit leaves the sender
and counts against the interaction budgets of \cref{sec:compute,sec:connect}. The \emph{decoder cost}
lands on a battery-powered headset or phone. \emph{Viewport dependence} decides whether the stream can be
cut down to what the user will see, and therefore whether prediction and prefetching
(\cref{sec:anticipate}) have anything to act on.

We group representations into four families by what they send (\cref{tab:encode-families}).
\emph{Image-based} representations send what cameras see and reuse the 2D video chain.
\emph{Geometry-based} representations send the shape of the scene as points or meshes.
\emph{Learned} representations send a model of the scene, or a description of its meaning, and let the
receiver regenerate the content. \emph{Signal-based} representations send a physical signal, force or
light, sampled by the rules of the sense that receives it. \Cref{fig:encode-chains} follows a chain from
each family from capture to display. Adaptation and caching are treated in \cref{sec:anticipate}, haptic
transport and holoportation in \cref{sec:connect}.

\subsection{Image-based: send what a camera sees}
Image-based representations keep the 2D video chain intact and inherit its hardware encoders and
decoders. Their questions sit at the two ends of that chain: how the scene is mapped onto rectangles, and
how many decoders the client can afford.

\emph{Projected 360-degree video} assumes a viewer who turns the head but stays in place (3DoF), and wins
when that holds. The established result is that concentrating bits on the viewport saves bitrate, but
less than it saves pixels: Oculus's offset cube used fewer than half the pixels of an equirectangular
frame for similar view quality, yet saved only 5.6--16.4\% of bitrate \cite{W2623027537}. The delivery
unit is contested. Viewport-specific versions need no reassembly but multiply storage. Tiles share one
encode but cost 7.3--10.4\% in coding efficiency \cite{W2896840651}, up to 37.3\% for small tiles at low
resolution \cite{W2623784065}, and the client pays for them: Flare raised phone CPU load from 18\% to
58\% \cite{W2896840651}, and streaming took 28.5\% of headset power \cite{W2626466913}, both on
2017--2018 hardware. Gaze is the stronger lever: gaze-based quantisation saved 40--47\% in cloud VR gaming in a subjective test \cite{W3174397927},
and gaze-contingent compression was five times more effective when motion-to-photon (MTP) latency stayed
below 15~ms \cite{W4214531162}. Foveation is thus a coding gain that \cref{sec:compute} pays for in
latency.

\emph{Multi-view plus depth} sends several colour and depth streams and lets the receiver synthesise the
view, which gives limited six degrees of freedom (6DoF) with ordinary video codecs. It assumes the
receiver can afford view synthesis and the viewer stays within the span the cameras covered. It wins in
live, two-way systems because it needs no slow 3D encoder. Project Starline sends four colour and three
depth H.265 streams at 30--100~Mbit/s and fuses them at the receiver, because real-time mesh or
point-cloud compression was not possible at its resolution \cite{W4200107448}; MetaStream likewise codes
RGB-D as 2D video \cite{W4387212733}. MPEG Immersive Video (MIV)
standardises this path, with atlas packing \cite{W4210296418} and view synthesis at the decoder
\cite{W3034756801}. Practice and evidence diverge: deployed telepresence uses this family, yet no study
has streamed MIV to users over a real network, and light-field delivery has been tested only offline
\cite{W2902432774}.

\subsection{Geometry-based: send the shape}
Geometry-based representations send the scene itself, so the receiver can render any viewpoint. The price
is weight. Raw point clouds need 720~Mbit/s at 200K points and 30~fps \cite{W3021314748}, and about
5~Gbit/s at one million points \cite{W4362496249}, one to three orders of magnitude more than 2D video.

Point-cloud codecs trade compression ratio against speed, and the trade is established. V-PCC projects
patches onto planes and reuses video codecs; it reaches lossy ratios of 100--500 but took about 4~hours
to encode 30 frames of one object \cite{W3096788322}. G-PCC and Draco code geometry in 3D, run fast and
compress less, so live systems avoid V-PCC. In VR2Gather, the MPEG Anchor codec cut 281.8~Mbit/s to
19.0~Mbit/s but raised latency from 40.6 to 278.7~ms, and parallel tiles brought it back to 90.6~ms
\cite{W4362496249}: coding, not transit, set the delay. Tuning standard codecs yields single-digit gains
(2--6\% BD-rate from V-PCC rate control \cite{W4405304599}); larger gains come from changing the
representation, as when 3D sub-sampling cut bitrate by up to 48.5\% at equal quality \cite{W4379806044}
and 3D patch coding matched V-PCC's ratio with real-time decoding \cite{W4379928158}. The receiver is the
second constraint: Hermes reached 30~fps on phones only by exploiting inter-frame correlation
\cite{W4387967921}. No codec wins everywhere; a measurement of Draco, G-PCC, V-PCC and two neural codecs
found that the best choice depends on content and viewing behaviour \cite{W4321488303}.

Geometry's advantage is a richer viewport dependence: points can be culled by visibility, occlusion and
distance, not only by direction. ViVo saved 40\% of bandwidth on average with SSIM above 0.99, but only
10\% for phone users on the same content \cite{W3021314748}, and gaze-driven delivery on a HoloLens~2
saved up to 67\% \cite{W4399323898}. Meshes are the less explored member: static mesh codecs have been
benchmarked for rate and runtime \cite{W2911819174}, and a reconstructed mesh for robot teleoperation
needed 14~Mbit/s on average \cite{W2966611292}.

\subsection{Learned: send a model or a meaning}
Learned representations replace explicit content with something the receiver must regenerate. They
promise the largest savings and move the most work to the two ends.

\emph{Neural scene representations} (NeRF, 3D Gaussian splatting) send model parameters. The most
deployable design reuses existing hardware: V$^3$ bakes dynamic Gaussians into 2D videos for H.264
decoders and reaches 32.97~dB PSNR at 0.532~MB per frame and 27~fps on a phone \cite{W4404525293}. That
rate is not small: sustained at 30~fps it is about 128~Mbit/s, the range of multi-view video, so the gain
is rendering freedom rather than bitrate. Training takes about a minute per frame on a desktop GPU, which
rules out live content, and download was measured only on an internal network. Layered Gaussians
\cite{W4402704690}, variable-bitrate NeRF video \cite{W4400667199} and splat streaming at 30~fps on
phones \cite{W4416477288} point towards adaptation; error control and quality assessment remain open
\cite{W4408847683}.

\emph{Semantic and generative codecs} send meaning and assume that sender and receiver share a knowledge
base or a generative model. They report the largest savings and the weakest comparisons. WiserVR sent
1.81~Mbit instead of 28.3~Mbit for 20 frames, in an AWGN simulation that excluded the delay of sharing the
knowledge base \cite{W4324296656}; MagicStream reports up to 1195$\times$ less bandwidth
\cite{W4404034698}. The one generative prototype timed end to end cut bandwidth by 99.93\% but raised
latency from 980~ms to 13.66~s \cite{W4403677254}. Each paper defines fidelity differently, task metrics can disagree with perceived realism
\cite{W4385452934}, and the baseline is usually BPG with LDPC rather than HEVC or VVC.
An order-of-magnitude saving is plausible; its price in inference delay and device energy is assumed
rather than measured.

\subsection{Signal-based: send the physical signal}
Signal-based representations code a physical quantity by the limits of the sense that receives it.
Haptic and holographic content sit at opposite ends of the rate scale.

\emph{Haptic signals} need little rate and strict timing. Kinaesthetic samples travel at 1~kHz or more to
keep the control loop stable, and perceptual deadband coding sends a sample only when its change exceeds a
Weber fraction (7--15\% for force), removing 80--90\% of packets without perceptible loss. Two
independent implementations of the IEEE~1918.1.1 kinaesthetic codec reproduced the same rates and errors
\cite{W2912555763}, an established result, though for one contact point. Vibrotactile
coding is open-loop and may use block transforms with tens of milliseconds of delay \cite{W2911218692}.
Learned multichannel codecs report cuts of 80--92\%, but against each other rather than a standard
\cite{W7131889935,W4406137775}. Deadband coding also makes traffic bursty, unlike the
periodic 1~ms flow that transport studies assume.

\emph{Holographic content} has no agreed representation, and its requirement figures depend on an unstated
one. Akyildiz and Guo define a hologram as a recorded light field, then adopt point clouds as the working
form; their light-field display example needs 221~GB per frame, or 6.63~TBps at 30~fps
\cite{W4300960574}. Measured systems used 5--100~Mbps for compressed point clouds over live 5G
\cite{W4312662428} and 173~Mbps for full-duplex light-field telepresence \cite{W3176472668}. The figures
do not estimate one quantity: one is uncompressed display arithmetic, the other compressed capture. For
live use, codec delay binds first: V-PCC needs 11--42 minutes to encode one second \cite{W4309030584}.
Optical holograms are only now becoming coded media \cite{W4417448513}.

\paragraph{The choice moves cost; it does not remove it.} No representation is cheap at both ends. Image-based content is light on the sender and heavy on the wire
and the decoders. Geometry cuts the wire at the price of encoder delay. Learned
representations cut it further and move the work into training and inference. Choosing a representation is therefore choosing where to
spend: on the network, the sender's encoder or the receiver's processor, and the choice cannot be made
alone. Remote rendering (\cref{sec:compute}) collapses any representation back into 2D video at the
edge, trading decoder cost for MTP latency. Viewport dependence bounds what prediction can save
(\cref{sec:anticipate}), and encoder delay decides which representations two-way telepresence can use
(\cref{sec:connect}). The ratio--speed trade within each family is established; that new representations win
across families is assumed, since none has been compared with the others at matched quality.

\begin{openproblems}
\item \textbf{Which representation minimises end-to-end cost for a given scene?} Point clouds, meshes,
  multi-view video and Gaussian splats have never been compared on the same content at matched perceived
  quality. Encoding shared scenes in all four and streaming them over replayed 5G and Wi-Fi traces would
  measure bitrate, encoder delay and decoder load together.
\item \textbf{Do semantic and generative savings survive latency?} Reported savings come from AWGN
  simulations with weak baselines. Comparing semantic codecs with HEVC, VVC and V-PCC at matched rated
  quality on a real 5G link, timing encoder and decoder inference, would show whether they suit
  interactive use.
\item \textbf{How should new representations be judged?} No rating data exist for compressed Gaussian,
  neural or generative reconstructions viewed in a headset, so PSNR and LPIPS are unvalidated for their
  artefacts. A 6DoF rating study across codecs and rates would provide ground truth.
\item \textbf{Can codecs track a mobile link?} Rate control is tuned at fixed rates, and neural codecs lack
  bitrate ladders and loss handling. Rate control driven by measured capacity, judged by frame-deadline
  misses, would test whether codec gains hold on cellular links.
\item \textbf{What does a hologram actually cost to send?} Requirement tables rest on display arithmetic,
  not on captured content. Coding one scene as point clouds, splats, light fields and phase holograms and
  recording the traffic would replace assumed figures with measured ones.
\item \textbf{How do coded haptic streams behave under real loss?} Haptic codecs are evaluated on one-off
  recordings with one contact point. Shared multi-point recordings and a user study of deadband and
  vibrotactile codecs over replayed network impairments would test perceptual transparency where it
  matters.
\end{openproblems}
