\section{Where Computation Happens: Offloading, Remote Rendering and Latency}\label{sec:compute}

A headset has a few watts of power and a few milliseconds of slack. Offloading spends the second to
save the first: it moves rendering or perception to an edge or cloud server and pays in network delay,
which must still fit inside the deadline of the display loop, the motion-to-photon (MTP) latency. The
central design variable is therefore the \emph{split point}, the stage at which the sense--render--display pipeline
leaves the device. It fixes what crosses the network, which direction carries the load, how stale the
result is when it returns, and which corrections the device can still apply before display
(\cref{fig:compute-pipeline}). This section follows four decisions: where to split, how to budget the
delay a split adds, how to hide that delay, and what the split costs the device.

\subsection{Where to split}

The later the split, the less the device computes and the more bandwidth and staleness it inherits. Four
families of design follow from this tension (\cref{tab:compute-splits}).

\emph{Full remote rendering} moves the whole renderer and streams finished frames; the device keeps only
decoding, reprojection and display. Compression is mandatory: raw stereo frames at 2160$\times$1200 and
90~Hz need 5.6~Gbps \cite{W2838572662}. The design is simple and content-agnostic, but the codec sets a
floor on delay.

\emph{Split or hybrid rendering} divides the scene. Near or interactive objects are rendered on the device
and the distant scene remotely; one such system used 20--30\% of the bandwidth of full remote rendering
and tolerated delays of up to 200~ms \cite{W4389296467}. Others send an intermediate that the device can
re-render from a new pose: colour plus depth for client-side warping \cite{W2751899584}, or one
eye rendered remotely and the other reprojected, which lowered MTP by 36\% \cite{W4391557896}. For neural scenes, streamed as pixels or as dynamic
Gaussians \cite{W7106240991}, the right split is not yet known (\cref{sec:encode}).

\emph{Perception offload} is the AR counterpart. The device streams camera frames upward, an edge GPU
detects objects, and only labels and object poses return; tracking and overlay rendering stay local. The
load now sits on the uplink. Once the server has a GPU, the network dominates inference latency, and
sending frames as H.264 video rather than as images delivered about ten times more frames at 100~kbps
\cite{W2792220137}.

\emph{Placement} decides which server hosts the offloaded stages, and proximity is the one result that
holds across methods. In a shipyard deployment, a fog gateway and a cloudlet answered in about 1~ms
against 49.3~ms for a remote cloud, but the fog gateway was four times slower than the cloudlet at 240
clients, and the Wi-Fi hop was excluded by design \cite{W2805833065}. Static
placement breaks down once many users share an edge, because server queueing then dominates
\cite{W4213053765}. Adaptive controllers jointly optimise computation and communication, for example
with deep reinforcement learning \cite{W3120265095}. Their weakness is their inputs: a 2018 fit of
detector accuracy against frame size \cite{W2790412025} reappears in later formulations
\cite{W4388068949}. Learned placement is plausible but almost wholly untested on real render times and
real links.

\subsection{How to budget delay}

A latency budget is a sum of stage delays, and it means something only when it names the loop it bounds.
The common decomposition allows under 1~ms for sensing and about 5~ms for display within a 15--20~ms MTP
bound, which leaves about 14~ms for computing and communication \cite{W2784711283}. This is tight: a phone took 9--10~ms to encode and 14--16~ms to decode an H.264 frame
\cite{W2940859338}, so decoding alone can exceed the network's share.

Pipelining and timing matter as much as link rate. Cutting the Cord split each eye's frame across four
parallel encoders and aligned rendering with the client's display refresh. Over 802.11ad it reached
10--14~ms end to end against a sequential median above 26~ms \cite{W2838572662}. In AR the uplink is the largest stage. In multi-user AR over LTE, median end-to-end latency was 12.5~s, the radio
access network contributed 31.2\%, and uplink bursts triggered TCP slow starts \cite{W3047439371}; on 5G
mmWave the uplink was still insufficient for baseline edge detection \cite{W4390905995}.

Two loops are routinely conflated. MTP runs from head motion to the updated image; interaction latency
runs from a user's action to its visible consequence. In a crane simulator, display delays of 0--30~ms
affected performance, whereas controller delay mattered only at 800~ms \cite{W3086540722}. Quoted targets
range from 5~ms \cite{W3048503162} to 100~ms \cite{W2898344194}. The spread is not noise: it reflects
which loop is meant, and whether reprojection or local tracking already absorbs part of the delay.

Budgets are set on the mean, while users feel the tail. The 3GPP XR traffic model underestimated
effective bitrate by 25\% on one headset \cite{W4399340506}, so a budget met on average can still miss
many frames. Latency studies seldom report percentiles or jitter, although a phone camera can measure delay to within
10~ms \cite{W3012329178}. How jitter is perceived and
how transport shapes it are treated in \cref{sec:assure,sec:provision}.

\subsection{How to hide delay}

Every hiding technique lets the last stage on the device correct a result computed for an earlier state,
so it can correct only what the device observes locally. Four families differ in what that is.

\emph{Reprojection} (time-warp) shifts the last received frame to the newest head pose just before
display. It is the established success. Post-render warping removed up to 80\% of the aiming penalty of
80~ms added latency \cite{W3089399051}, and stalls of up to four frames at 90~Hz went unnoticed
\cite{W4301184864}. With reprojection active, ALVR users hardly noticed round-trip times up to 90~ms
\cite{W3095344018}, although CloudXR quality fell from 75~ms \cite{W4402156718}. Its limit is
fundamental: it corrects the viewpoint, not the scene. Moving objects, disoccluded regions and the outcome
of a user's action remain stale.

\emph{Prediction and pre-rendering} render for where the user will be. Rendering beyond the field of
view, sized to the current latency, reduced delivery latency by about 22\% in a simulation driven by real
user data \cite{W3161249521}. Such methods trade bandwidth for margin, and the prediction horizon bounds
their gain (\cref{sec:anticipate}). \emph{Speculation} goes further and pre-renders every reachable state:
Furion prefetched panoramas for each grid point at about 132~Mbps \cite{W2940859338}. It works only where
the state space is small.

\emph{Local tracking} hides the staleness of remote perception. Without it, 16.7~ms of offload latency
raised false detections from 0 to 31.56\%; with tracking and region-of-interest encoding, offload latency
also fell from 34.56 to 17.23~ms on 2.4~GHz Wi-Fi \cite{W2965289829}. CloudAR held 30 frames per second
with at least 97\% recognition accuracy \cite{W2765872303}. Local tracking is the established design rule of edge AR.

\emph{Hybrid fallback} keeps interactive objects on the device, so the delay-sensitive part never crosses
the network \cite{W4389296467}. No family hides loss: ALVR users rated packet loss as worse than added
delay \cite{W3095344018}, so delay hiding offers no protection on an unreliable link. Nor can any family hide
the state of other users (\cref{sec:connect}).

\subsection{What it costs the device}

Device energy is the main argument for offloading and its most contested result. Models promise savings
of up to 85\% \cite{W4388068949}, but measurements disagree. Edge offloading halved smartphone energy for
AR on average \cite{W2983318720}, whereas a Quest Pro drained its battery 15\% faster with wireless
offloading than with local rendering \cite{W4362489903}, and Air Link on a Quest~2 also cost more energy
\cite{W4387913063}. DeepDecision measured about 2900~mW for offloading regardless of bitrate and frame
rate \cite{W2792220137}, whereas most models scale energy with transmitted data. The models omit the radio's
tail energy and the power of the decoder and display. Offloading moves load from the GPU to the radio and
the decoder, so the saving depends on device, codec and link. None of these studies reports heat, so the
thermal trade-off, which also bounds sustained local rendering, is unmeasured.

\begin{openproblems}
\item \textbf{Which split point is best for a given scene, device and link?} Frame, layer, depth and
  Gaussian splits are each evaluated against their own baseline. A common benchmark with fixed scenes,
  devices and network traces would rank them.
\item \textbf{Do learned placement controllers survive real render times and real links?} They are
  trained on synthetic task sizes and channels. Replaying measured render times, head motion and 5G or
  Wi-Fi traces would show which gains hold.
\item \textbf{When does offloading save device energy?} Models predict large savings; headset
  measurements found the opposite \cite{W4362489903,W4387913063}. Component-level measurements of radio,
  decoder, display and GPU across headsets, codecs and links would settle the direction.
\item \textbf{How should a budget account for the tail and for interaction?} Budgets target mean MTP,
  while users feel late frames and delayed consequences of actions. Studies varying the delay distribution
  and the affected loop, with reprojection on and off, would yield derived budgets.
\item \textbf{Can edge AR scale on a shared uplink?} Real-network evidence is single-user and
  pessimistic \cite{W4390905995,W3047439371}. A 5G or Wi-Fi testbed scheduling several AR camera uplinks
  in one cell would test whether perception offload scales.
\end{openproblems}
