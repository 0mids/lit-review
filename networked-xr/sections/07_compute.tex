\section{Where Computation Happens: Offloading, Remote Rendering and Latency}\label{sec:compute}

A headset has a few watts of power and a few milliseconds of slack. This section asks which stages of
the perception--render--display pipeline should run on the device, at the edge or in the cloud, and how
the delay that each split adds is budgeted, measured and hidden. It draws on four atlas cells:
computation offloading and remote rendering (\nxrNProbOffloadingRemoteRendering{} papers,
\nxrShProbOffloadingRemoteRendering{} of the corpus), rendered frames (\nxrNContRenderedFrames{}
papers, whose share rose from \nxrShContRenderedFramesEarly{} to \nxrShContRenderedFramesLate{} with
\nxrCagrContRenderedFrames{} annual growth against \nxrCagrTotal{} for the corpus), AR objects and
overlays (\nxrNContArObjectsOverlays{} papers, whose share fell from \nxrShContArObjectsOverlaysEarly{}
to \nxrShContArObjectsOverlaysLate{}), and latency (\nxrNProbLatency{} papers, growing
\nxrCagrProbLatency{} a year). The two problem cells differ in method. Offloading papers divide between
real testbeds (\nxrRealOffloadingRemoteRendering{}) and simulation (\nxrSimOffloadingRemoteRendering{});
\nxrNoEvalOffloadingRemoteRendering{} report no evaluation and \nxrUsersOffloadingRemoteRendering{}
involve users. Latency papers are more empirical (\nxrRealLatency{} real testbed,
\nxrUsersLatency{} with users), yet \nxrNoEvalLatency{} report no evaluation. Radio scheduling and
transport are treated in Section~\ref{sec:provision}, and QoE models in Section~\ref{sec:assure}.

\subsection{Choosing the split}

Proximity is the one placement result that holds across methods. In a shipyard deployment, a fog
gateway and a cloudlet both answered in about 1~ms, against 49.3~ms for a remote cloud; the fog gateway
won for payloads up to 128~KB, the cloudlet above, and at 240 clients the fog gateway was four times
slower than the cloudlet, with the Wi-Fi hop excluded by design \cite{W2805833065}. A simulation found
edge execution cut AR latency by 41.44\% against local and 12.85\% against cloud execution
\cite{W2894645340}. What leaves the device varies (\cref{fig:compute-pipeline}). AR systems offload
perception and render locally (S1). VR systems offload rendering and stream frames (S2). Hybrid
designs render near or interactive objects on the device and the rest remotely (S3); one such design
used 20--30\% of the bandwidth of full remote rendering and tolerated delays of up to 200~ms
\cite{W4399444696,W4389296467,W3080913128}. Neural scenes reopen the question: current systems stream
pixels \cite{W4415672032}, dynamic Gaussians \cite{W7106240991} or intermediate level-of-detail results
\cite{W7117245976}, each against its own baseline.

Shared edges add contention, and this is where evidence thins. Once many users offload, queueing at
the edge server dominates \cite{W4213053765,W4399320016}, yet schedulers for concurrent rendering
sessions on one GPU exist only in simulation \cite{W3214338661,W4285254743}. The largest strand,
joint computation--communication optimisation, formulates offloading as task graphs solved by deep
reinforcement learning \cite{W3120265095} or Lyapunov control \cite{W4392567309}. More than nine in
ten learning-based offloading controllers are evaluated only in simulation, and their inputs are
assumed: a 2018 YOLO accuracy fit \cite{W2790412025} reappears in later formulations
\cite{W4386702674,W4388068949}, one paper sets 1~Mbit per frame \cite{W2608841173}, and VR streams
are modelled at a constant 25.32~Mbps \cite{W2596760110}.

Device energy, the main reason to offload, is contested. Models promise large savings: 63\%
\cite{W2608841173} and 85\% \cite{W4388068949}. Measurements disagree with each other. Edge offloading
halved smartphone energy for AR on average \cite{W2983318720}, and an edge VR testbed lowered client
power \cite{W4296339041}. A Quest Pro, however, drained its battery 15\% faster with wireless
offloading than with local rendering \cite{W4362489903}, and Air Link on a Quest~2 also cost more
energy than local execution \cite{W4387913063}. DeepDecision measured about 2900~mW for offloading
regardless of bitrate and frame rate \cite{W2792220137}, which contradicts energy models that scale
with transmitted data. Radio tail, decoder and display power, omitted from most models, are the
likely cause.

\subsection{Remote rendering: frames, codecs and frame pacing}

Compression is mandatory, and its cost is set by pipelining and timing more than by link rate.
Raw stereo frames at 2160$\times$1200 and 90~Hz need 5.6~Gbps. Cutting the Cord split each eye's frame
across four parallel hardware H.264 encoders and aligned rendering with the client's display refresh;
over 802.11ad it reached 10--14~ms end to end, against a sequential median above 26~ms, and cut missed
display refreshes from 5.3--14.3\% to 0.1--0.2\% \cite{W2838572662}. On phones the budget is
tighter. Furion measured 9--16 frames per second for local rendering, 9--10~ms to encode and
14--16~ms to decode H.264 frames, and 27\% of the phone's CPU spent on packet processing at
400~Mbps \cite{W2940859338}. Removing memory copies across the stack brought OpenUVR to 14.32~ms
\cite{W3124215200}, whereas WebRTC render streaming on Wi-Fi~6 batches packets at fixed intervals and
wastes airtime \cite{W4391506055}.

The renderer knows what the encoder must otherwise estimate, and using it pays. Object-aware
quantisation with gaze saved 40--47\% bandwidth in a subjective test \cite{W3174397927}. Foveated
shading alone did not reduce bitrate, while foveated post-processing or encoding did
\cite{W3186601312}; optimisers that treat foveation as a compute knob ignore this distinction
\cite{W4391341842}. Some systems change what is streamed: colour plus depth for client-side warping
\cite{W2751899584}, object-space shading atlases \cite{W2903169695}, one eye rendered remotely and the
other reprojected with 36\% lower MTP \cite{W4391557896}, and reuse of pixels across frames with 95\%
less frame data \cite{W2928088097}. Each uses its own baseline, so the representations cannot be
ranked (Section~\ref{sec:encode}). Two assumptions remain untested. No corpus paper measures AV1 or
VVC latency for rendered frames; the only mention is a position paper \cite{W4391267710}. Frame sizes
are bursty even under constant-bitrate coding \cite{W4379468406}, and on one headset the 3GPP baseline
model underestimated effective bitrate by 25\% \cite{W4399340506}.

\subsection{AR offloading: results, overlays and the uplink}

AR offloading succeeds only if the device hides the staleness of remote results. The replicated
design runs a light tracker on the device between remote detections. In one system, without
tracking, 16.7~ms of offload latency raised false detections from 0 to 31.56\%; with tracking and
region-of-interest encoding, offload latency fell from 34.56 to 17.23~ms on 2.4~GHz Wi-Fi and
detection accuracy rose by 20.2--34.8\% \cite{W2965289829}. CloudAR held 30 frames per second with at
least 97\% recognition accuracy \cite{W2765872303}, Jaguar about 33~ms end to end \cite{W2896416526},
and the same pattern holds on HoloLens \cite{W4282961825} and in MARVEL \cite{W2898344194}. Once the
server has a GPU, the network dominates: sending frames as H.264 video rather than images delivered
about ten times more frames at 100~kbps \cite{W2792220137}.

The uplink is the bottleneck. In multi-user AR over LTE, median end-to-end latency was 12.5~s, the
radio access network contributed 31.2\%, and uplink bursts triggered TCP slow starts
\cite{W3047439371}. A measurement on 5G mmWave found its uplink insufficient for baseline edge
detection \cite{W4390905995}, while vision papers assume 5G solves the problem \cite{W3133913697}.
Result sharing among co-located users cut latency by up to 130$\times$ \cite{W4213053765}, but the
overlap it relies on has never been measured. Targets are contested: below 20~ms \cite{W2609285553},
below 50~ms \cite{W3133913697} and below 100~ms \cite{W2898344194,W4282961825} are all quoted. Which one
applies depends on whether overlays are world-locked and tracked locally, and no study maps network
delay to registration error. The one controlled study found users' ratings poorly calibrated to their
actual performance \cite{W7168371891}.

\subsection{Budgeting, measuring and hiding delay}

Latency budgets are inherited rather than derived. The common figure is 15--20~ms MTP, leaving about
14~ms for computing and communication \cite{W2784711283}; others assume 13~ms \cite{W2561715836} or
5~ms \cite{W3048503162}, and one survey lists 5, 7, 20 and 40--300~ms \cite{W4362585626}. Users are
more tolerant than these figures suggest once reprojection is active. CloudVR users did not notice
20~ms of added network latency \cite{W2896076858}, and ALVR users hardly noticed round-trip times up
to 90~ms \cite{W3095344018}, whereas CloudXR quality fell from 75~ms \cite{W4402156718}. In a crane
simulator, display delays of 0--30~ms mattered while controller delay mattered only at 800~ms
\cite{W3086540722}. Budgets therefore depend on which loop is meant and on the hiding technique, and
offloading papers rarely state either.

Measurement is cheap but rarely done. A photosensor rig \cite{W2890615649} and a phone-camera method
with under 10~ms uncertainty \cite{W3012329178} both measure MTP directly, yet fewer than one in
twenty latency papers report jitter or tail latency, the quantities that cause judder.

Latency hiding is the established success. Post-render warping removed up to 80\% of the aiming
penalty of 80~ms added latency \cite{W3089399051}. Rendering beyond the field of view, sized to the
current latency, reduced delivery latency by about 22\% against simpler placement in a simulation with
real user data \cite{W3161249521}. Motion prediction lets the edge pre-render frames: LSTM and MLP
predictors were trained on more than 840,000 samples \cite{W3094082851}, and head-motion prediction
serves cloud volumetric rendering \cite{W3032301564}. Reprojection has moved from rotation to
translation \cite{W4408848620}, and selective time-warp leaves head-locked layers untouched
\cite{W2908115098}. Short stalls of up to four frames at 90~Hz went unnoticed, and reprojection helped
from eight frames upward \cite{W4301184864}. Each method, however, is tested on its own motion data
and with fixed delays, so their gains cannot be compared.

\begin{openproblems}
\item \textbf{Learning-based offloading controllers are untested on real traces.} More than nine in
  ten are evaluated in simulation with synthetic task sizes and channels. An open benchmark replaying
  measured render times, head motion and 5G/Wi-Fi traces under 3GPP XR traffic would show which gains
  survive.
\item \textbf{Device energy savings are modelled, not measured.} One Quest Pro drained 15\% faster
  with offloading \cite{W4362489903}; one edge testbed lowered power \cite{W4296339041}. Component-level
  measurements of local and remote rendering across headsets, codecs and links would settle the
  direction.
\item \textbf{3GPP XR traffic models and delay budgets go unused and unvalidated.} No offloading paper
  evaluates against them, and the one empirical check found a 25\% bitrate underestimate
  \cite{W4399340506}. Commercial split-rendering traces fitted against the models are needed.
\item \textbf{Rendered-frame streams lack error resilience.} Users rank loss above delay
  \cite{W3095344018,W4404916373}, yet forward error correction, intra refresh and concealment for
  remote rendering barely appear in the corpus. A loss-trace study with user ratings would close this.
\item \textbf{No benchmark couples motion traces, network traces and MTP ground truth.} Warping and
  prediction methods each use their own data and fixed delays. A shared dataset with photodiode MTP
  would let them be ranked.
\item \textbf{The AR camera uplink is unstudied at multi-user scale.} Real-network evidence is
  single-user and pessimistic \cite{W4390905995,W3047439371}. A 5G testbed scheduling several AR
  uplinks in one cell would test whether edge AR scales.
\end{openproblems}
