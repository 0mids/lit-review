\section{A Functional Framework for Networked XR}\label{sec:framework}

Networked XR is usually described by its parts: latency, caching, 5G, point clouds, holographic
communication. These labels mix levels. Latency is a property, caching a mechanism, 5G a technology and
holographic communication an application. Lists of such topics therefore do not show how the parts
depend on each other. We instead describe the field by \emph{function}, the job a part of the system
performs in the loop between the user's body and what the user perceives. Each function answers one
design question that any networked XR system must answer, whatever its medium or access network.
\Cref{fig:architecture} places the six functions on a reference architecture, and \cref{tab:framework}
summarises the families of approaches that each function contains.

\paragraph{The six functions.}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=2pt,font=\normalfont\bfseries]
  \item[Encode] (\cref{sec:encode}). \emph{What representation of the scene travels, and how is it
        compressed?} Projections and tiling of 360-degree video, point clouds and meshes, multi-view
        video, neural scene representations, holograms and haptic signals.
  \item[Anticipate] (\cref{sec:anticipate}). \emph{What will the user need next, and how is it delivered
        in time?} Viewport-adaptive streaming, head, gaze and 6DoF motion prediction, and caching and
        prefetching.
  \item[Compute] (\cref{sec:compute}). \emph{Where does each stage of perception and rendering run, and
        how is the added delay budgeted and hidden?} Offloading, remote and split rendering, AR inference
        at the edge, motion-to-photon budgets, reprojection and device energy.
  \item[Provision] (\cref{sec:provision}). \emph{How do radio, scheduling, transport and architecture
        supply the rate, delay and reliability required?} Resource allocation, mmWave, terahertz,
        reconfigurable surfaces and Wi-Fi, congestion control and loss resilience, and 6G and metaverse
        architectures.
  \item[Connect] (\cref{sec:connect}). \emph{How are several people and remote machines kept consistent
        and responsive?} Multi-user synchronisation, avatars, haptic teleoperation, holographic
        telepresence and digital twins.
  \item[Assure] (\cref{sec:assure}). \emph{How do we know the result is good enough, and safe?} QoE
        measurement and modelling, traffic characterisation, and security, privacy and trust.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Why functions.} The grouping has three useful properties. First, each function is
\emph{one decision}. Encoding chooses a representation and anticipation a fetch schedule. Computing
chooses a split point and provisioning an allocation. Connecting chooses a consistency model and assuring
a definition of ``good enough''. Approaches that answer the same decision can be compared directly, even
when they come from different communities. Second, the functions are \emph{ordered along the loop}.
Encoding and anticipation decide what is sent, computing and provisioning decide where and how, and
connecting and assuring concern the people at the ends. Third, the functions are \emph{coupled}, and the
couplings are where most design difficulty lies.

\paragraph{How the functions couple.} Two couplings run through the whole review. The first is the
\emph{shared deadline}. A head-worn display tolerates about 15--20~ms from motion to photon, which
leaves roughly 14~ms for computation and communication \cite{W2784711283}. Every function spends from
this one budget. A longer prediction horizon in anticipation relaxes the network but costs accuracy. A
remote split in computing adds a round trip that provisioning must keep short and that reprojection must
partly hide. A heavier codec in encoding saves rate but adds encoding delay. The second coupling is the
\emph{trade between network and device}. Almost every design moves cost from one to the other. A compact
representation must be decoded or synthesised on the headset. Rendering at the edge saves the headset's
GPU but spends its radio and battery, and prefetching saves delay but spends bandwidth on content that
may never be seen. Reading the sections that follow with these two couplings in mind explains why
results rarely transfer between settings. A gain obtained under one budget, or one division of cost, is
not a gain under another.
