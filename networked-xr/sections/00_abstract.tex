\begin{abstract}
\noindent Extended reality (XR) asks more of a network than any earlier medium. It needs hundreds of
megabits per second for 360-degree and volumetric content, a motion-to-photon deadline of a few tens of
milliseconds, a continuous uplink of pose and sensor data, and a world that several people share. This
review maps the research on meeting these demands. It draws on the Networked XR Atlas: \nxrIncluded{}
papers published between October 2016 and October 2026, screened from \nxrHarvested{} OpenAlex records,
labelled on five dimensions and read in depth where they mattered most.

We organise the field by the six things a networked XR system must do: \emph{encode} the scene,
\emph{anticipate} what the user will need, decide where to \emph{compute}, \emph{provision} the network,
\emph{connect} people and machines, and \emph{assure} experience and trust. For each function we report
what is established, what is contested and what is merely assumed.

Three findings stand out. First, the field has moved from delivering content to allocating resources.
Work on anticipation (adaptive streaming, prediction, caching) fell from \fnAnticipateEarly{} to
\fnAnticipateLate{} of output between 2017--19 and 2022--24, while work on provisioning the network rose
from \fnProvisionEarly{} to \fnProvisionLate{}. Second, evidence has
weakened as the field has grown. Evaluation on real networks or testbeds fell from \nxrEvalRealEarly{} to
\nxrEvalRealLate{} of papers. Over the same period, simulation and analysis rose from \nxrEvalSimAnaEarly{}
to \nxrEvalSimAnaLate{}, and only \nxrSynLearnedReal{} of learning-based papers were tested on real
networks. Third, network metrics are seldom tied to what users perceive. Requirement figures are
copied from survey to survey rather than measured, and the uplink, multi-user scale and the cost of
security are rarely studied. We close with nine cross-cutting gaps and a research agenda ordered by what
each project unlocks for the others.
\end{abstract}
