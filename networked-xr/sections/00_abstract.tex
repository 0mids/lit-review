\begin{abstract}
\noindent Extended reality (XR) asks more of a network than any earlier medium. A head-mounted display
closes a perceptual loop between the user's body and the image: when the head turns, the new view must
appear within a few tens of milliseconds. Once content, computation or other participants are elsewhere,
the network sits inside that loop. It must carry 360-degree and volumetric media at hundreds of megabits
per second, return rendered frames inside a motion-to-photon deadline, take a continuous uplink of pose
and sensor data, and keep several people and machines consistent with each other.

This review organises networked XR by the six things every such system must do. It must \emph{encode}
the scene into something that can be sent, and \emph{anticipate} what the user will need next. It must
decide where to \emph{compute}, and \emph{provision} the network that carries the result. It must
\emph{connect} people and machines that share a world, and \emph{assure} that the experience is good
enough and safe. For each function we set out the design question, group the approaches into families
by their underlying idea, and separate what has been established on real systems from what is contested
and what is merely assumed.

Read together, the functions expose a common structure. Each is a trade between network and device: a
leaner representation costs decoding, a longer prediction horizon costs accuracy, and moving rendering
off the headset costs delay that must be hidden. The functions are also coupled through a shared
deadline, so a decision in one sets the budget of the next. Across all six, the same weaknesses recur.
Objectives are rarely validated against what users perceive, and requirement figures are inherited
rather than measured. Controllers are seldom tested beyond the simulator, and the uplink, multi-user
scale and the latency cost of security are studied least. We close with a dependency-ordered research
agenda.
\end{abstract}
