\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A head-mounted display shows a world that must respond to the head that wears it. When the user turns,
the new view must appear within a few tens of milliseconds, or the image lags the body and comfort
fails \cite{W2784711283}. On a standalone device this deadline is met locally. Once the scene, the
computation or the other participants are elsewhere, the network becomes part of the perceptual loop.

Extended reality (XR), the umbrella term for virtual, augmented and mixed reality, therefore asks more of
a network than video or the web, in four ways. The first is \emph{rate}. A 360-degree or volumetric
scene sent whole needs hundreds of megabits per second, although the user sees only a fraction of it at
any moment \cite{W3038754372,W3096564590}. The second is \emph{deadline}. Remote rendering must return a
frame within the motion-to-photon (MTP) budget, so delay is not merely annoying but perceptually
disqualifying \cite{W2784711283}. The third is \emph{direction}. The device sends pose, camera frames and
captured media upstream all the time, so XR is not a download. The fourth is \emph{sharing}. When several
people inhabit one world, or one person operates a remote machine, the network must keep endpoints
consistent with each other, not just deliver media to each \cite{W2561715836}.

Research on these demands is scattered across communities that seldom read each other. Multimedia-systems
researchers built viewport-adaptive streaming \cite{W3038754372,W2971680322}. Mobile-computing
researchers offloaded AR recognition to the edge \cite{W3133913697}. Wireless researchers framed XR as a
joint allocation of radio, computing and energy. Haptics researchers pursued the tactile internet
\cite{W2811040154}, and 6G and metaverse visions placed all of it inside a future network
\cite{W4312940001,W4390204075}. Existing surveys follow these boundaries. Each covers one medium, such as
360-degree video \cite{W3038754372,W2971680322,W2917103012}, point clouds \cite{W3096564590} or haptics
\cite{W3088582486}, or one technology, such as edge computing \cite{W3133913697,W2902319104}, ultra-low
latency \cite{W4362585626} or the 6G metaverse \cite{W4312940001,W4315497913}. What is missing is a single
frame that shows how these parts fit together, where they trade against each other, and how far each has
been tested. \Cref{tab:surveys} places representative surveys against the six functions used in this
survey.

\paragraph{Organising idea.} We organise the field by \emph{function}: the six things any networked XR
system must do, whatever its medium and access network (\cref{sec:framework}). A system must
\emph{encode} the scene into something that can be sent (\cref{sec:encode}). It must \emph{anticipate}
what the user will look at or do next, so that the right content arrives in time (\cref{sec:anticipate}).
It must decide where to \emph{compute}, on the device, at the edge or in the cloud, and how to hide the
delay this adds (\cref{sec:compute}). It must \emph{provision} the network with links, schedulers,
transport and architecture that supply the rate, delay and reliability required (\cref{sec:provision}).
It must \emph{connect} users with each other and with remote machines, through shared state, touch and
telepresence (\cref{sec:connect}). And it must \emph{assure} that the result is good enough and safe for
the people who use it (\cref{sec:assure}). Each function poses its own design question. Within each, we
group approaches into a few families by their underlying idea, so that methods described in different
vocabularies can be compared on the same terms.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=(\arabic*)]
  \item A \textbf{functional framework} that places the problems of networked XR on one reference
        architecture and defines each function by a design question (\cref{sec:framework}).
  \item For each function, a \textbf{classification of approaches into families}, stating what each
        family assumes, when it wins and where its limits lie, with the measured results that support each
        claim (\cref{sec:encode,sec:anticipate,sec:compute,sec:provision,sec:connect,sec:assure}).
  \item An account of the \textbf{couplings between functions}: the shared deadline and the trades
        between network and device that tie them together.
  \item \textbf{Nine cross-cutting gaps} and a research agenda ordered by dependency
        (\cref{sec:synthesis,sec:agenda}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Organisation.} \Cref{sec:background} introduces the XR loop, the motion-to-photon deadline
and the properties of XR traffic for readers new to the area. \Cref{sec:framework} presents the
functional framework. \Cref{sec:encode,sec:anticipate,sec:compute,sec:provision,sec:connect,sec:assure}
treat the six functions in loop order, and each closes with its open problems. \Cref{sec:synthesis}
draws the cross-cutting gaps together, \cref{sec:agenda} proposes a research agenda, and
\cref{sec:conclusion} concludes.
