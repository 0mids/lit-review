\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Networked XR puts the network inside a perceptual loop. That single fact explains why its problems
differ from those of video streaming or cloud gaming. Content must be chosen before the user looks at
it. Rendering can move off the headset only if its delay can be hidden. Radio and transport must deliver
frames, not flows, on time. Several people and machines must agree on one world. And quality must be
judged at the human end, where both experience and privacy are decided.

Organising the field by function makes its structure visible. Six design questions cover it: what to
encode, what to anticipate, where to compute, how to provision, how to connect and how to assure. Each
has a small number of families of answers. Two couplings tie the functions together. The first is a
shared deadline that every function spends from. The second is a trade between network and device that
almost every design shifts one way or the other. Results rarely transfer between settings because they
were obtained under a particular budget and a particular division of cost.

The weakest links are not algorithms but shared ground. The field lacks open, frame-synchronised traces
of real XR workloads, and a model of experience fitted to people under realistic network dynamics. With
these two artefacts, the questions that recur in every section become answerable on common terms: whether
a learned scheduler beats a heuristic on a real cell, how much latency a defence costs, and what a
hologram really needs. Without them, networked XR will keep producing designs faster than it can tell
which of them work.
