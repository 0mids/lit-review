\section{Connecting People and Things: Shared Worlds, Touch and Telepresence}\label{sec:connect}

The previous sections treat one user and one stream. This section asks how networks keep several users, their bodies and remote machines consistent with each other. The function holds \fnConnectN{} papers (\fnConnectSh{} of the corpus), and its share of output fell from \fnConnectEarly{} in 2017--19 to \fnConnectLate{} in 2022--24. Five cells make it up: multi-user synchronisation (\nxrNProbMultiuserSynchronisation{} papers, \nxrShProbMultiuserSynchronisation{}), avatars and social state (\nxrNContAvatarsSocialState{}, \nxrShContAvatarsSocialState{}), tactile and haptic transport (\nxrNProbTactileHapticTransport{}, \nxrShProbTactileHapticTransport{}), holographic communication (\nxrNProbHolographicCommunication{}, \nxrShProbHolographicCommunication{}) and digital-twin data (\nxrNContDigitalTwinData{}, \nxrShContDigitalTwinData{}). Their paths diverge. Tactile transport fell from \nxrShProbTactileHapticTransportEarly{} to \nxrShProbTactileHapticTransportLate{} of the corpus as the Tactile Internet agenda faded, while digital-twin content grew fastest, at \nxrCagrContDigitalTwinData{} per year. Synchronisation and holographic papers are mostly built systems (\nxrRealMultiuserSynchronisation{} and \nxrRealHolographicCommunication{} on real testbeds), yet only \nxrUserStudyMultiuserSynchronisation{} of synchronisation papers are controlled user studies. Haptic transport is the reverse: \nxrSimTactileHapticTransport{} simulation and \nxrUsersTactileHapticTransport{} with users. \Cref{fig:connect-arch} places the five strands in one session and sorts their loops into three kinds: a control loop that can lose stability, interactive loops bounded by perception, and freshness loops bounded by the user's decision. Haptic and holographic coding belong to \cref{sec:encode}.

\subsection{Shared state: consistency, scale and fairness}

The design question is how much of the shared world each client must receive, and how fresh. Deployed platforms answer bluntly. Five commercial social VR platforms forward every avatar update unprocessed, so throughput, latency and client load rise roughly linearly with users; two-user end-to-end latency was 101.7--239.1~ms \cite{W4307020344}. A controlled study located the limit in replication, not link capacity: update intervals grew from 18~ms with 2 clients to 158~ms with 125, and perceived fluidity collapsed beyond 25 avatars \cite{W2922194619}. The largest shared world in the corpus had 225 clients \cite{W4400277138}, against metaverse definitions that demand thousands \cite{W4312940001}. Interest management and dead reckoning, the classical remedies of networked virtual environments, appear in only \nxrSynXSyncInterestN{} papers, none evaluated with users. This is the clearest case in the atlas of a problem solved in games that XR has not reused.

Consistency is traded against latency by assumption. Augmented Virtual Teleportation left its 1.2~s live video unsynchronised with avatar state to stay responsive \cite{W3005948101}, whereas two users actuating one digital twin without a policy conflicted in 33--56\% of windows \cite{W7168268649}. No study measures which inconsistencies users notice. In co-located AR the shared coordinate frame is the bottleneck: SPAR cut spatial inconsistency by up to 60\% \cite{W3110126341} and edge map merging finished within 200~ms \cite{W4311118480}, yet anchor transfers took 12.7--87.18~s \cite{W4411499419}. Synchronisation faults are also the most common symptom in 2,649 bug reports from multi-user XR software \cite{W7166703064}.

Fairness is the most contested question. Of \nxrSynXSyncSkewN{} delay-skew schedulers, \nxrSynXSyncSkewSim{} are simulations; they assume that equal delay means equal experience \cite{W4285149407}. Predictive buffering cut inter-client skew by 76\% for 100 clients, in a controlled testbed \cite{W7168249167}. The user evidence points elsewhere: dyads perceived network impairment but completed tasks no slower \cite{W4392600587}. The largest controlled study used 30 pairs \cite{W7163944086}, and a review of 56 studies found almost all optimise latency for an individual, not a group \cite{W7154321690}. Group quality of experience (QoE) beyond two users is unmeasured.

\subsection{Representing remote people: from pose streams to holoportation}

Remote people travel as parameters, semantics or geometry, and the choice spans four orders of magnitude in rate. Parametric avatars are cheap: 24~B per update for a head pose, 216~B for a 17-joint skeleton \cite{W2922194619}, and 31--83~kbps for two users on VRChat, Rec Room and Hubs \cite{W4307020344}. Expressiveness multiplies the rate: Horizon Worlds exceeded 4.5~Mbps downstream at 15 users \cite{W4307020344}. Apple's spatial personas send semantic data at about 0.7~Mbps but lack rate adaptation and cap a call at five \cite{W4403981191}. Geometry is costly. An operator trial streamed live point clouds at 5--100~Mbps, with end-to-end latency near 170~ms when phones decoded and 70~ms when the edge did \cite{W4312662428}; depth packed into H.264 needed 3--30~Mbps \cite{W4307263396}. Semantic drivers sit between: Mon3tr animated a Gaussian avatar with under 0.2~Mbps at about 80~ms \cite{W7123904049}.

Reusing the 2D real-time stack is established. A WebRTC selective forwarding unit for point clouds adapted faster than TCP \cite{W7151958829}, and L4S converged on bandwidth 45\% faster than Google congestion control without frame loss \cite{W4408870658}. Whether 3D beats 2D is contested. Project Starline \cite{W4200107448} and stroke consultations with 30 outpatients \cite{W4400580381} favour 3D; a floating-head prototype \cite{W7127277161} and a VR2Gather--Zoom comparison \cite{W4415708482} rated 3D below 2D video. Every one of these studies held the network fixed, although humanoid avatars lost more QoE under network distortion than cartoon ones \cite{W4415821547}. Two assumptions go unexamined. Semantic schemes report savings in PSNR, never against geometry at equal bitrate with users. Two-way calls are treated as downlink problems, yet only two papers measure the holographic uplink \cite{W4210904516,W4416047836}, and the operator trial attached its camera by Ethernet \cite{W4312662428}.

\subsection{Touch across the network: haptic loops and teleoperation}

Haptic teleoperation is the only loop here that closes a control system, so delay threatens stability as well as experience. The field set its requirement early at 1~ms end-to-end latency with 99.999\% reliability \cite{W2216406802}, and IEEE 1918.1 fixed a master--network--slave reference architecture \cite{W2911218692}. The 1~ms target appears in \nxrSynOneMsN{} abstracts, only \nxrSynOneMsUsersN{} of them evaluated with users. Measured tolerance is far looser and depends on the controller. Switching between time-domain passivity (TDPA) and model-mediated teleoperation (MMT) held QoE at delays up to 200~ms \cite{W4200154132}, and users noticed short haptic delays but compensated \cite{W3162230828}. The requirement belongs to the controller, not to the network alone.

Compensation gives the clearest gains. Co-designing receiver-side prediction with radio resources saved up to 77.80\% bandwidth \cite{W4321488300}, and octree-based MMT raised force accuracy by 44\% with fewer than half the model updates \cite{W4401416073}. Both rest on open-loop or synthetic trajectories; no predictor has been tested in closed loop with a live network, a stable controller and users. Transport results split by medium. A time-sensitive networking testbed kept high-priority flows at dedicated-wire quality under 200\% over-saturation \cite{W4387917733}, whereas Wi-Fi needs MAC changes that reached a 3.72~ms worst case for Wi-Fi~7 only in simulation \cite{W4205674533}. Haptic streams rarely travel with their video: no paper co-schedules them with remote-rendered frames. Cross-modal work even questions the usual priority of haptics, since deliberate haptic loss with visual restoration raised opinion scores by more than 80\% at 30\% loss \cite{W4401507901}. The largest multi-user haptic study had six pairs \cite{W4413393831}.

\subsection{Digital twins: keeping physical state fresh}

A digital twin reverses telepresence: the network must keep a replica fresh enough for the user's decision. Two communities answer differently. Metaverse-framed work optimises age of information (AoI), latency and reliability for short packets \cite{W4366251214}; optimal-transport association cut synchronisation time from 31.23--51.23~ms to 19.11--25.17~ms \cite{W4387870504}. These studies assume their workloads, such as 10~Mb per twin update \cite{W4387870504}, and fewer than one in ten use a real testbed, against nearly two in three of other twin papers.

The prototype community reports what networks deliver. A robot vehicle mirrored on a headset updated in 29.63~ms over a local network \cite{W7213228059}, but middleware dominates elsewhere: 416~ms per transmission for a robot-arm VR twin \cite{W4417338851} and 1,015~ms end to end for a cobot through a commercial IoT platform \cite{W7168263637}. Every figure was called adequate. Compact state beats pixels: pose streaming kept remote work usable under added latency where video did not \cite{W4312316324}, and server push used three orders of magnitude less bandwidth than polling for a 5,005-building city twin \cite{W7172377742}. Freshness metrics have multiplied, yet none has been tested against what a user perceives or decides.

\begin{openproblems}
\item \textbf{Group QoE and fairness are unmeasured.} \nxrSynXSyncSkewSim{} of \nxrSynXSyncSkewN{} skew schedulers are simulated, and controlled studies stop at pairs \cite{W7163944086}. Groups of two to four under per-user netem impairments, with a shared task score and perceived fairness, would give schedulers a validated target.
\item \textbf{No state-stream traces exist.} Avatar, twin-update and haptic streams have no public traces or traffic models, so simulations assume sizes and arrival processes \cite{W4387870504,W4321488300}. Packet-level traces from instrumented open-source platforms, fitted to a 3GPP-style model, would make schemes comparable.
\item \textbf{Haptic tolerance is assumed, not measured.} The 1~ms target persists while users tolerated 200~ms with adaptive control \cite{W4200154132}. Factorial delay, loss and jitter studies across control schemes and XR tasks would yield tolerance curves for provisioning.
\item \textbf{Haptics travels without its video.} No paper co-schedules haptic flows with remote-rendered frames. Joint scheduling on a shared Wi-Fi or 5G link, measuring haptic p99.9 delay, motion-to-photon latency and inter-stream skew, would close this gap.
\item \textbf{Holographic calls lack a conversational QoE model.} All 3D-versus-2D studies hold the network fixed, and semantic avatars were never compared with geometry at equal bitrate. A two-way call study varying delay, loss and uplink bitrate against a matched 2D control is needed.
\item \textbf{Twin freshness is not tied to user decisions.} AoI is optimised, yet no study links staleness to anomaly detection or control errors. Psychometric staleness curves for monitoring tasks would turn freshness budgets from guesses into requirements.
\end{openproblems}
