\section{Connecting People and Things: Shared Worlds, Touch and Telepresence}\label{sec:connect}

The previous sections treat one user and one stream. This section asks how networks keep several users, their bodies and remote physical systems consistent and responsive with each other. Five atlas cells answer it: multi-user synchronisation (\nxrNProbMultiuserSynchronisation{} papers, \nxrShProbMultiuserSynchronisation{} of the corpus), avatars and social state (\nxrNContAvatarsSocialState{}, \nxrShContAvatarsSocialState{}), tactile and haptic transport (\nxrNProbTactileHapticTransport{}, \nxrShProbTactileHapticTransport{}), digital-twin data (\nxrNContDigitalTwinData{}, \nxrShContDigitalTwinData{}) and holographic communication (\nxrNProbHolographicCommunication{}, \nxrShProbHolographicCommunication{}). Their trajectories diverge. Tactile transport fell from \nxrShProbTactileHapticTransportEarly{} to \nxrShProbTactileHapticTransportLate{} of the corpus as the Tactile Internet agenda lost momentum. Digital-twin content grew fastest, at \nxrCagrContDigitalTwinData{} per year, and holographic communication grew at \nxrCagrProbHolographicCommunication{}. The evaluation profiles also diverge. Synchronisation and holographic papers are mostly built systems (\nxrRealMultiuserSynchronisation{} and \nxrRealHolographicCommunication{} on real testbeds), but only \nxrUserStudyMultiuserSynchronisation{} of synchronisation papers are controlled user studies. Haptic transport is the reverse: \nxrSimTactileHapticTransport{} simulation, \nxrNoEvalTactileHapticTransport{} without evaluation and \nxrUsersTactileHapticTransport{} with users. Haptic coding and holographic representations are treated in \cref{sec:encode}; here the concern is the loops that connect participants. \Cref{fig:connect-loops} shows these loops and the delay each one is known to tolerate.

\subsection{Shared state: consistency, scale and fairness}

The design question is how much of the shared world each client must receive, and how fresh it must be. Deployed platforms answer it bluntly. Measurements of five commercial social VR platforms show throughput, end-to-end latency and client load rising roughly linearly with the number of users, because servers forward every avatar update unprocessed; two-user end-to-end latency was 101.7--239.1~ms \cite{W4307020344}. A controlled study located the limit in replication rather than link capacity: update intervals grew from 18~ms with 2 clients to 158~ms with 125, and perceived fluidity collapsed beyond 25 avatars \cite{W2922194619}. The largest shared-world demonstration in the corpus had 225 clients \cite{W4400277138}, against metaverse definitions that require thousands per session \cite{W4312940001}. The classical remedies of networked virtual environments, interest management and dead reckoning, appear in only \nxrSynXSyncInterestN{} corpus papers, none evaluated with users. This is the clearest case in the atlas of a solved problem from games that XR has not reused.

Consistency is traded against latency, and the trade is made by assumption. Augmented Virtual Teleportation deliberately left its 1.2~s live 360-degree video unsynchronised with avatar and object state to stay responsive \cite{W3005948101}, whereas digital-twin work found conflict windows of 33--56\% when two users actuate one twin without a policy \cite{W7168268649}. No study measures which inconsistencies users notice. In co-located AR, the shared coordinate frame is the bottleneck. SPAR cut spatial inconsistency by up to 60\% and latency by up to 55\% \cite{W3110126341}, and edge map merging completes within 200~ms \cite{W4311118480}, yet anchor transfers take 12.7--87.18~s \cite{W4411499419} and multi-user AR on public LTE had a 12.5~s median end-to-end latency \cite{W3047439371}. Synchronisation faults are also the most common symptom in 2,649 bug reports from multi-user XR software \cite{W7166703064}.

Fairness is the most contested register. Of \nxrSynXSyncSkewN{} delay-skew and fairness schedulers, \nxrSynXSyncSkewSim{} are simulations and one was tested with users; they assume that equalising inter-player delay equalises experience \cite{W4285149407}. Predictive buffering cut inter-client skew by 76\% for 100 clients, but in a controlled testbed \cite{W7168249167}. The most direct user evidence points the other way: dyads perceived network impairment but completed tasks no slower \cite{W4392600587}. The largest controlled study used 30 pairs \cite{W7163944086}, and a review of 56 studies found that almost all optimise latency for an individual rather than a group \cite{W7154321690}. Group quality of experience (QoE) for more than two users is therefore unmeasured, and no scheduler has a validated group target.

\subsection{Representing remote people: from pose streams to holoportation}

Remote people can be sent as parameters, as semantics or as geometry, and the choice spans four orders of magnitude in rate. Parametric avatars are cheap: per-avatar updates range from 24~B for a head pose to 216~B for a 17-joint skeleton \cite{W2922194619}, and two-user data channels on VRChat, Rec Room and Hubs ran at 31--83~kbps \cite{W4307020344}. Expressiveness multiplies the rate. Horizon Worlds used 752/413~kbps up/down and exceeded 4.5~Mbps downstream at 15 users \cite{W4307020344}; Horizon Workrooms added 0.5~Mbps per participant \cite{W4294643390}. Apple's spatial personas send compact semantic data at about 0.7~Mbps, below 2D video calls, but have no rate adaptation and cap a call at five personas \cite{W4403981191}. Geometry is costly: an operator trial placed live point clouds at 5--100~Mbps, with end-to-end latency of about 170~ms when phones decoded and 70~ms when decoding moved to the edge \cite{W4312662428}; depth packed into H.264 needed 3--30~Mbps \cite{W4307263396}. Semantic drivers sit between: Mon3tr animated a pre-built Gaussian avatar with under 0.2~Mbps at about 80~ms \cite{W7123904049}, and MagicStream reported up to 1195$\times$ less bandwidth than point clouds \cite{W4404034698}.

What is established is that reusing the 2D real-time stack works. A WebRTC selective forwarding unit for point clouds adapted 13\% and 36\% faster than TCP at 5 and 10~ms latency \cite{W7151958829}, and L4S converged on bandwidth 45\% faster than Google congestion control without frame loss \cite{W4408870658}. Multipoint units reduce client load as sessions grow \cite{W4387969165}, though only small single-site sessions have been tested. What is contested is whether 3D beats 2D. Project Starline \cite{W4200107448} and stroke consultations with 30 outpatients \cite{W4400580381} favour 3D, while a floating-head prototype \cite{W7127277161} and a VR2Gather--Zoom comparison \cite{W4415708482} rated perceptual quality or ease below 2D video. Every one of these studies held the network fixed. Fidelity also interacts with impairment: humanoid face-tracked avatars lost more QoE under network distortion than cartoon avatars \cite{W4415821547}. Two assumptions go unexamined. Semantic schemes claim savings measured in PSNR, never against geometry at equal bitrate with users. Two-way calls are treated as downlink problems, yet each participant must upload the same stream, and only two papers measure the holographic uplink \cite{W4210904516,W4416047836}; the operator trial attached its camera by Ethernet \cite{W4312662428}. Avatar streams also leak input: motion recovered from packets inferred 97.62\% of keystrokes \cite{W4398796475} (\cref{sec:assure}).

\subsection{Touch across the network: haptic loops and teleoperation}

Haptic teleoperation is the only loop in this section that closes a control system, so delay threatens stability as well as experience. The field framed its requirement early as 1~ms end-to-end latency at 99.999\% reliability \cite{W2216406802}, and IEEE 1918.1 fixed a master--network--slave reference architecture \cite{W2911218692}. The 1~ms target appears in \nxrSynOneMsN{} corpus abstracts, only \nxrSynOneMsUsersN{} of them evaluated with users. Measured tolerance is far looser and depends on the control scheme. With switching between time-domain passivity and model-mediated teleoperation (MMT), QoE held at delays up to 200~ms \cite{W4200154132}, and users noticed short haptic delays but compensated for them \cite{W3162230828}. The requirement is therefore a property of the controller, not of the network alone.

Control-theoretic compensation is where the clearest gains lie. Task-oriented co-design of receiver-side prediction and radio resources saved up to 77.80\% bandwidth, and receiver-side prediction outperformed transmitter-side prediction on lossy links \cite{W4321488300}. Octree-based MMT raised force accuracy by 44\% with fewer than half the model updates \cite{W4401416073}. These results rest on open-loop or synthetic trajectories, however: the co-design study drew its rare events from 2$\times$10$^{11}$ generated samples \cite{W4321488300}. No predictor has been evaluated in closed loop with a live network, controller stability and users. Radio theory adds a hard limit: when the queuing-delay bound falls below the channel coherence time, the required transmit power becomes unbounded \cite{W2963776947}.

Transport results split by medium. Deterministic Ethernet protects haptic flows: a time-sensitive networking testbed kept high-priority streams at dedicated-wire quality under 200\% over-saturation \cite{W4387917733}, and a P4 queue manager cut their latency by at least 60\% \cite{W4324031645}. Wi-Fi needs MAC changes, which reached a 3.72~ms worst case for Wi-Fi~7 \cite{W4205674533} and up to 82\% lower latency for Wi-Fi~6 \cite{W4313886724}, but only in simulation. Haptic streams rarely travel with the video they accompany. The cell has no paper on remote-rendered frames against 34 expected, and no study applies L4S to haptic traffic. Cross-modal work questions the usual priority of haptics over video: deliberate haptic packet loss with visual restoration raised opinion scores by more than 80\% at 30\% loss \cite{W4401507901}. Quality metrics remain unsettled, as objective signal errors disagree with user ratings \cite{W4283390118}, and the largest multi-user haptic study had six pairs \cite{W4413393831}.

\subsection{Digital twins: keeping physical state fresh}

A digital twin reverses the direction of telepresence: the network must keep a virtual replica fresh enough for the user's decision. Two communities answer differently. Metaverse-framed work treats synchronisation as an optimisation of age of information (AoI), latency and reliability for short packets \cite{W4366251214}. Optimal-transport association reduced synchronisation time to 19.11--25.17~ms from 31.23--51.23~ms \cite{W4387870504}, and AoI-aware policies cut traffic and energy in simulation \cite{W7169800215}. These studies assume their workloads, such as 10~Mb per twin update \cite{W4387870504} or channel gains drawn from a unit distribution \cite{W4383753323}. Fewer than one in ten metaverse-framed twin papers use a real testbed, against nearly two in three of the remaining twin papers.

The prototype community reports what networks deliver. State updates take tens of milliseconds on local and private networks: 29.63~ms for a robot vehicle mirrored on a headset \cite{W7213228059}. Middleware and cloud APIs dominate the delay, reaching 416~ms per transmission for a robot-arm VR twin \cite{W4417338851} and 1,015~ms end to end for a cobot through a commercial IoT platform \cite{W7168263637}. Every one of these figures was called adequate. Compact state beats pixels: pose streaming kept remote work usable under added latency where video did not \cite{W4312316324}, a persistent twin with transient object updates enabled teleoperation at kbps rates \cite{W4405784937}, and server push used three orders of magnitude less bandwidth than polling for a 5,005-building city twin \cite{W7172377742}. Freshness metrics have multiplied (AoI and several twin-specific ages) without any being tested against what a user perceives or decides. Twin state is never adapted to throughput, and network twins of XR networks remain visions, with a single XR interface evaluated \cite{W4393174725}.

\begin{openproblems}
\item \textbf{Group QoE and fairness are unmeasured.} \nxrSynXSyncSkewSim{} of \nxrSynXSyncSkewN{} skew schedulers are simulated, and controlled studies stop at pairs \cite{W7163944086}. Groups of two to four under per-user netem impairments, with a shared task score and perceived fairness, would give schedulers a validated target.
\item \textbf{No state-stream traces exist.} Avatar, twin-update and haptic streams have no public traces or traffic models, so simulations assume sizes and arrival processes \cite{W4387870504,W4321488300}. An open corpus of packet-level traces from instrumented open-source platforms, fitted to a 3GPP-style model, would make schemes comparable.
\item \textbf{Haptic tolerance is assumed, not measured.} The 1~ms target persists while users tolerated 200~ms with adaptive control \cite{W4200154132}. Factorial delay, loss and jitter studies across control schemes and XR tasks would yield tolerance curves for provisioning.
\item \textbf{Haptics travels without its video.} No paper co-schedules haptic flows with remote-rendered frames. Joint scheduling on a shared Wi-Fi or 5G link, measuring haptic p99.9 delay, motion-to-photon latency and inter-stream skew, would close this gap.
\item \textbf{Holographic calls lack a conversational QoE model.} All 3D-versus-2D studies hold the network fixed, and semantic avatars were never compared with geometry at equal bitrate. A two-way call study varying delay, loss and bitrate against a matched 2D control is needed.
\item \textbf{Twin freshness is not tied to user decisions.} AoI is optimised, yet no study links staleness to anomaly detection or control errors. Psychometric staleness curves for monitoring tasks would turn freshness budgets from guesses into requirements.
\end{openproblems}
